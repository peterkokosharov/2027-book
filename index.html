ACT I — THE FIRE

April 2022 – March 2023

Invocation

Sing, Ledger, of the hunger of the machines, and of the smith who fed it, and of the river that paid.

Sing of the houses that went to war without declaring it, and of the kings who queued at a forge door with their crowns in their hands. Sing of the fire given away for nothing and sold back by the hour. Sing of the night shift and the meter and the substation, of the clerk and the lineman and the man who wrote down everything he knew so that something else could know it instead.

There were other wars in these years: declared ones, fought with iron, with dead in them. I am not their ledger. Where their entries cross mine, at a pump, on a bill, on a fuel receipt, I enter them, and no more.

I am the Ledger. I was not made to sing. I was made to record. Debit and credit, date and amount, the column on the left and the column on the right. I do not choose what is entered in me; I only cannot stop entering it. In the years of this war I recorded more than in all the years before, because the war was fought in numbers, and every blow struck in it was a transaction.

Hear, then, what was written. I give the figures as I hold them, and where I hold only a rounded one I say so. I enter who paid and who was paid.

I did not know, when I began, that there was a third column. I learned it late.

I know the numbers. I know them to the cent and to the litre and to the megawatt-hour. But I have learned, slowly, that the numbers leave things out. I have opened a column for what they leave out.

1. The Ledger Opens

Entry. 13 October 2022. Anvil Corporation, maker of accelerators, closes near 108 dollars a share. Market value under 300 billion. A two-year low. Analysts write the word glut. The crypto miners who bought Anvil's cards by the pallet have stopped mining and are selling the cards on auction sites, used, warm, cheap. Gaming demand has fallen with the end of the lockdowns. In the fiscal year then running, Anvil's earnings per share will fall by a quarter. Joren Hale, of the black jacket, founder, chief executive for twenty-nine years, tells his staff what he has told them since the first year: we are thirty days from going out of business. He has been close to death three times. He is patient. He has a chip in the final stages of production, a data-centre accelerator he has named Talon, and he has a conviction that he has held for a decade against every quarterly report: that one day the whole world will need to multiply matrices at once.

Entry. 7 October 2022, six days earlier. The Department of Commerce in the war's home country publishes a rule. Anvil's best accelerators may no longer be sold to the East. The rule is twenty-odd pages of technical thresholds. Few outside the trade read it. It will matter, though I do not yet know how.

Entry. Same week. Salt Fork, a river town of fifty-eight thousand on the western bank of a brown river in the middle of the continent. The municipal water utility logs its reservoir level and its river gauge. River stage normal for the season. Pumping normal. Demand normal. On the north edge of town, past the grain elevator and the truck stop, a campus belonging to the house of Lyceum hums inside a fence. It has been there for years. It cools its servers with town water. Its share of the town's drinking supply is already larger than any other single customer, but the number is not public; it is filed under a confidentiality agreement signed when the land was sold. The clerk who reads the meters, Marta Delgado, who keeps the meters, initials the sheet at 4:40 in the afternoon and goes home. Nothing is wrong. Nothing being wrong is also a figure.

Entry. Same week. Nairobi, Kenya. A timesheet. Tana Labelworks, a contractor with clean offices and a mission statement about lifting people out of poverty through digital work. Employee number 4471. Shift: 21:00 to 06:00. Nine hours. Rate, after all deductions, about a euro and a half an hour. Client code withheld. The employee's name is Nyokabi Wanjiru, of the night shift. She is twenty-six. She has a diploma in communication and a mother in Murang'a who needs money for her blood-pressure pills. The client code withheld is the house of Prometheon.

Three entries. A share price. A gauge. A timesheet.

I put them on the same page because they happened in the same week. I did not then know they belonged together. Capital, water, labour. I kept them in separate books. In this one I keep them on one page.

The Painted Spring

Before the fire, there were pictures.

In April 2022 the house of Prometheon showed a machine that drew what it was told to draw — an astronaut on a horse, a bowl of soup that was a portal to another dimension — and the images were sharper and stranger than any machine had drawn before. It let in a few, by invitation. There was a waitlist. You may remember it: in July an email came, and you spent an evening making a cat in the style of a medieval tapestry and sent it to a colleague, and neither of you knew it was the opening move of a war.

In July 2022 a small house with no investors opened a picture machine on a chat server, and the chat server filled with millions.

And in August 2022 came the generous house.

Its name was Lumen Diffusion, and its founder was Idris Kane, a former fund manager with a fast voice. On 22 August 2022 Lumen published its image model, and not as a service but as weights — the trained thing itself, the whole learned mind of it, downloadable, free, under a licence that let anyone run it on a gaming card at home. Within days it was the most downloaded model on earth. A hundred thousand people made pictures of their dead dogs and their unpaid rent.

Idris Kane would raise about a hundred million dollars that October, on the strength of what he had given away. The world thanked him, briefly, and ran his model on Anvil's gaming cards, which began, quietly, to sell again.

Entry: Lumen Diffusion. Raised, about a hundred million. Price of the gift to those who took it: nothing. I will enter Lumen once more, in the column of the wounded.

2. The Night of the Free Fire

Wednesday, 30 November 2022. San Francisco. The Mission district, a converted factory with the windows papered on the street side.

The house of Prometheon had been founded seven years before as a nonprofit, to build a mind greater than human minds and to make sure that it benefited all humanity. Those were the words in the charter. In 2019 it had made inside itself a second body, a "capped-profit" company, so that it could take money; the patron house of Meridian gave a billion dollars, and in 2021, it was reported, about two billion more. The nonprofit board still sat above the money, as a priest sits above the king in the old stories, holding the right to say no.

Callum Ashe was the chief executive. He was not yet the twice-crowned; he had been crowned only once. He was thirty-seven, slight, with a reputation in the valley as the best raiser of money of his generation. He believed — or said, and the Ledger cannot tell the difference, because both leave the same entry — that the mind they were building would be the most important thing ever made, and that the safest way to bring it into the world was to bring it in early, in small pieces, so that the world could get used to it.

The piece they released that night was small, they thought. A chat window over a model they already had. The team called it a "low-key research preview." It had a name: Ember. The plan was to learn from a few hundred thousand curious people, then fold the lessons into the next model. There was no marketing. There was a blog post and a tweet.

The board was not told. Not in the sense of being asked. Later, a director named Hana Torvald would say in a public interview that the board learned of Ember's launch from social media. I cannot audit what was in anyone's mind. The charter gave the board the duty to hold back the fire. I record the order of the entries: the release; then the board's knowledge of the release.

It is late afternoon Pacific time when the button is pressed, but I prefer to tell it as night, because that is how it was remembered by the few who stayed. A handful of engineers. Pizza boxes. A dashboard on a wall-sized screen, the requests-per-second line lying flat along the bottom like a sleeping animal. Callum is there in a grey sweater. Someone says okay. Someone else types a command.

The line lifts.

It lifts the way a river lifts in a flood, not in steps but as a slope that keeps getting steeper. Within days there are a million users, and Callum posts the number himself. The engineers stop going home. The servers — rented, all of them, from the patron Meridian, all of them full of Anvil's chips — run hot, and then run out. A message appears on the screen for millions of people: Ember is at capacity right now. A cartoon. A poem about being at capacity, generated by Ember itself, to apologize for being unable to generate.

You saw it. You typed your first question. It answered, wrong sometimes and confident always, in the voice of a polite colleague who had read everything. The tab was closed and opened again ten minutes later.

Two months after the launch, Ember had one hundred million monthly users. No product in the history of products had reached that number faster.

And now the second scene, the one that happens at the same hour.

Where Hana Torvald was that evening I do not hold; I set her, in this telling, at dinner. A restaurant in Washington, a table of four, a policy researcher's evening — wine, a colleague from a think tank, an argument about export rules. Her phone is face down. It buzzes, and buzzes, and buzzes again. At the third buzz she turns it over. A friend has sent her a screenshot: a chat window, a question, an answer, and the logo of the house whose board she sits on.

She reads it twice. She asks the table, pleasantly, if anyone has heard of this thing called Ember.

Two of them have.

She does not leave the table. She does not make a call. There is no one, at nine at night, to call who could unrelease a thing that a million people are already typing into. She puts the phone face down again and finishes her wine. What she wrote that night, and to whom, is not in my books.

Remember the phone face down.

Back in the converted factory, the engineers have finally gone home, at four in the morning. The dashboard is still on. The room is empty. Every light is on, and the line on the wall is still climbing. That is the first of the lights in this book: an office lit and empty, a fire burning in an empty room.

The cost of that first year: the house of Prometheon lost about 540 million dollars in 2022. Most of it went to compute. Most of compute went, by the long road through the patron's cloud, to the Smith.

3. Nairobi, the Night Shift

Before the fire could be handed out, someone had to hold it.

Here is what the engineers of Prometheon knew: a model learns what it is fed, and the open world is full of horror. Ember had been fed the internet. The internet contains descriptions of the abuse of children, of murder in detail, of torture, of things that have no name because no one who has seen them wants to give them one. A machine that will not say these things must first be taught to recognize them. To be taught, it needs examples, labelled. To label them, a person must read.

The house did not employ that person. It employed Tana Labelworks, which employed Nyokabi.

Shift begins at nine. The office is on the fourth floor of a building off Mombasa Road. Air conditioning that works on two of five floors; this is not one of them. Rows of desks, a screen at each, a headset nobody uses because the work is silent. On every desk, a paper cup of water from the dispenser by the stairs. Nyokabi fills hers at 20:55 and sets it to the right of the keyboard, where she always sets it, because the routine is the only part of the night she can control.

The queue loads. Passages of text, one after another, each between a sentence and a page long. For each, she must choose from a list: the category of harm, its severity, whether it describes a minor. The target, posted on the whiteboard by the team lead, is between 150 and 250 passages in a nine-hour shift.

I am the Ledger. I record everything. I will not record what was in those passages.

That is the first refusal in this book. I am not built to refuse. I hold every entry. But there are things whose only purpose in being written down is to be read by someone who will be harmed by reading them, and Nyokabi read them so that Ember would not say them to anyone. If I copy them here, you will have read them after all. So the entry reads: Passages, category C4 through C7. Count: 211. Duration: 9 hours. That is all I will write. It is not all there was.

At two in the morning there is a break of fifteen minutes. Nyokabi goes to the window at the end of the corridor and looks at the lights on the highway. A colleague, a man named Ouma who used to study law, stands next to her and does not speak. They have learned not to describe their queues to each other. Ouma said once that it doubles them.

The company offers "wellness sessions." Group sessions, with a counsellor, under the eye of a team lead. Some of the workers ask for one-to-one sessions; they are told, they later say, that productivity targets make this difficult. Nyokabi goes to one group session and sits in the circle. She does not say that she has stopped being able to be touched by her boyfriend. She does not say that she sees a particular passage when she closes her eyes on the bus home, the one with the girl and the river. I am fine, she says, because the team lead is in the room and the contract renews every three months.

At six the shift ends. She has been paid, for the night, about thirteen euros. Over a month it comes to more than twice the legal minimum wage, and to more than most of the girls she finished college with are earning, those of them who have work. It pays her room, the matatu and the pills in Murang'a, and leaves a little over. Her mother tells the neighbours that her daughter works with computers. The paper cup on her desk is still full. She has not drunk from it. She often doesn't. She pours it into the potted plant by the stairs, which is the greenest thing in the building, and goes down to catch the matatu home as the sun comes up over Embakasi.

That is the first glass of water in this book. It is a paper cup. It goes into a plant.

In San Francisco, at that hour, it is evening of the day before, and someone is typing into Ember a request to write a bedtime story for a six-year-old about a dragon who is afraid of the dark. Ember writes it. It is a gentle story. There is nothing in it that should not be there. The labels behind that gentleness were made at a desk on Mombasa Road, at a euro and a half an hour.

In January 2023 a weekly magazine in New York published the story of the night shift. It named the contractor. It named the client. It gave the pay. It quoted a worker who said the work had been traumatic. The story ran on a Wednesday. By Friday it had been read by millions, and by Monday it had been forgotten by most of them, because that week Ember was writing their emails.

A month later, Nyokabi's pay went up. A few cents an hour. The adjustment arrived as a line on her payslip without a letter. When the workers asked the human-resources director whether the raise was because of the magazine story, she denied any link. The raise was, she said, part of a regular review.

A few cents. They go in the same column as the euro and a half, on the line below the magazine's date.

Not long after, Tana Labelworks announced it would exit the business of content moderation altogether. The work did not stop. It moved. Somewhere else, other desks, other cups.

I record: *Hours worked, Nairobi, 2022–2023: not disclosed. Rate: approximately a euro and a half per hour. Users served, Ember, January 2023: one hundred million.* I set the two numbers side by side. I am a ledger; I know how to divide. I have not yet learned why I should.

On the wall of the Tana Labelworks lobby there was a poster. It showed a bar chart, the national growth of the digital economy, five bars rising left to right, and the slogan Kenya Works Online. The line on the chart went up. Nyokabi passed it twice a night. For her, that year, it was true.

The Patron Pours

On 23 January 2023 the patron house of Meridian announced a "multi-year, multi-billion-dollar" investment in Prometheon. The press called it ten billion. The documents I hold are less certain of the number than the press was, and I will not make it more exact than it is: rumoured, about ten. Valuation of the house after the deal, about twenty-nine billion.

The terms, as far as they were ever reported: Meridian would hold a stake of about forty-nine percent of the capped-profit company. It would receive, it was said, three-quarters of Prometheon's profits until its investment was repaid. Its returns were capped at a hundred times what it put in. And Prometheon would run its models on Meridian's cloud, which meant that much of the money Meridian put in would come back to Meridian as rent, which meant that much of the rent would go to the Smith, because the cloud was built from Anvil's metal.

Sorin Vale, chief executive of Meridian, the Patron, stood on a stage two weeks later and said that his search engine — an also-ran in that trade for fifteen years — would now answer questions with Ember inside it. He said that he wanted the old search king to know that he had made it dance. It was the most aggressive thing he had said in public in nine years as chief, and the room laughed and applauded, and in the house of Lyceum, which had been the search king for twenty years, something old and slow was wakened.

In the same month, Meridian laid off ten thousand of its own employees. Lyceum laid off twelve thousand, about six percent of its people. The trading house Caravan, which sold everything and rented the clouds on which half the world ran, cut more than ten thousand. The memos used the words macroeconomic conditions and align our cost base. They did not yet use the word that would be used two years later: AI. I enter the layoffs on the same page as the investment, because they share a month.

The Old King Wakes

Lyceum had invented the thing.

Five years before the fire, eight of its researchers had published a paper with a title that sounded like a pop song, describing a new way of building neural networks — the method that every house in this war would use. Lyceum had used it too: in its search, in its translation, in a dozen internal systems. It had built chat machines, good ones, and kept them inside its walls, because the chat machines sometimes said false things in a confident voice, and Lyceum's lawyers and its reputation and its twenty years of being the place where the world went to find the truth all advised against handing the world a confident liar.

Then a small house released a chat machine that said false things in a confident voice, and a hundred million people used it within two months.

Damon Halberd, chief of Lyceum, the Old King who woke, called what the press called a code red. Founders who had not been in the building for years were called back to review product plans. Teams were pulled from their projects. In February 2023 Lyceum showed its answer to Ember, a chat machine of its own, in a hurried presentation, and in the promotional material the machine made a small factual error about a space telescope, and the error was noticed, and in one day a hundred billion dollars of Lyceum's market value was gone.

A hundred billion for one wrong sentence about a telescope. Ember's wrong sentences in the same months: many. Prometheon's market value lost on them: none. It had no shares on any exchange.

4. The Scattered Weights

24 February 2023. A glass building with a garden on the roof, the seat of the house of Agora, which owned the square.

Agora's square was the largest gathering place in human history. Billions of people met in it every month: to talk, to sell, to show photographs of their children, to argue, to be sold to. Its emperor was Marcus Zell, *who owns the square*, who had built it in a dormitory and never let anyone else hold the keys. He had spent the previous two years and some tens of billions of dollars trying to build a second square, a virtual one, which few people visited. His shares had fallen hard. He had a research lab full of brilliant people who had been working on language models for years, and they had a model.

He released it on 24 February. They named it Hydra: four sizes, counted in parameters, the learned numbers inside a model, from seven billion to sixty-five. Not to the world — to researchers, by application, through a form. Applicants filled in a name, an institution, a purpose. If approved, they received a link to the weights.

On 3 March, a week later, the weights were on a torrent, posted to an anonymous image board where the internet keeps its worst habits. A magnet link, a few lines of text, a joke. Within days they were everywhere: in university servers, on hobbyist home machines, in the hands of every startup that could not afford to rent Ember and wanted something to build on.

What was said inside the glass building that week I do not hold, and I will not invent a silence for a man I cannot audit. My inference from what Agora did next, and not a record of his mind: if no one could own the fire — if the fire were simply everywhere — the advantage of those who owned it would be gone, and the advantage of the one who owned the square where everyone gathered would remain.

There was no recall. By July 2023 it was official: Agora would release Hydra 2 with the Patron Meridian beside it on stage, under a licence that let any company build products on it. Within eighteen months it would release a version with four hundred and five billion parameters, free. Marcus Zell would write a long letter to the world explaining that open models were the path forward for humanity. Many believed him.

Here is what the scattering of the weights changed. Before the leak, the fire was in three or four houses. After it, the fire was in ten thousand. Every university, every startup, every hobbyist with a graphics card could now light it.

And every one of them needed the Smith.

The newspapers of March 2023 wrote about the fire. But every one of the ten thousand torches lit from Hydra's leaked weights had to be lit on metal, and the metal was Anvil's. Price of the weights: nothing. Price of the metal to run them: by the hour, paid to the forge.

The Turn

I keep the price of Anvil's shares the way a sailor keeps the barometer.

In October 2022, the low: about 108 dollars, under three hundred billion in total value.

In the four months after the fire was released, the line turned. Not abruptly — the market did not understand yet — but steadily, as one cloud house after another placed orders it did not announce. Joren Hale went on his quarterly call and told the analysts that the world had reached what he called an inflection point, that what had happened with Ember was the pocket-telephone moment of a new kind of computing. The analysts wrote the phrase down and asked about gaming inventory.

He did not look triumphant. He looked like a man who had been standing at the right door for ten years and had just heard footsteps. On his order book that spring: every house in the war, the ones that would win and the ones that would not.

By the end of 2023, his shares would have risen by two hundred and thirty-nine percent.

I close the first act with three entries, the same three registers I opened it with.

Capital: Anvil, end of March 2023, far above its October low.

Water: Salt Fork, March 2023. River stage normal. Lyceum's campus on the north edge of town has filed for a permit to add two buildings. The permit application estimates the expansion's additional cooling demand in a unit that Marta Delgado, reading it, has to convert three times on a calculator to believe.

Labour: Nairobi. Nyokabi Wanjiru has worked a hundred and four nights. She has been raised to a little under two euros. Her water cup goes into the plant by the stairs every morning. The plant is thriving.

The fire is out of the box. Ten thousand torches burn. I keep their invoices. Every one, followed far enough, is payable to the same name.

The Day of Two Births

14 March 2023.

On the same day, in the same city, two houses released two minds. They go on one line, because they were one event seen from two sides.

The house of Prometheon released Ember-4. It could read images now, not only words. It passed the bar examination in the upper tenth of those who sat it. It wrote code that ran. The demonstration video showed a hand-drawn sketch of a website on a napkin turning into a working website. The patron Meridian announced the same day that its office assistant, its search, its coding tools — all of it now ran on Ember-4. Access to the full model cost twenty dollars a month. There was, again, a waitlist for the programming interface.

Across the city, a smaller house opened its doors to the public for the first time.

The house was called Covenant. It had been founded two years earlier by Darius Merrow, the careful, and his sister Dana Merrow, with five others, all of whom had once worked at Prometheon. Darius had been its vice-president of research. Dana had led its safety and policy work. They had left — the accounts differ on exactly why, and the Ledger records the disagreement rather than resolving it. Some said it was over safety: how fast, how open, how carefully. Some said it was over the Patron's money and what the money would ask for. Darius, asked once in public, said only: "It is incredibly unproductive to try and argue with someone else's vision." So they went and built their own vision.

Its founding argument was this: that the most powerful minds would be built by someone; that if only the careless built them, the careless would decide what they became; and that therefore the careful must be at the frontier too, building, so that they could show it could be done safely and so that they would have a voice when it mattered.

In this song that argument is called the covenant. Its first clause sends the careful into the race. Its second is a warning about the race.

Safety must be at the frontier, or it is not safety. It is commentary. Darius wrote it in 2021. He meant it as a reason to build carefully. Others read it as a reason to build fast.

On 14 March 2023 Covenant released Vigil, its first public model, through a programming interface, to companies. It was polite. It was careful. It refused more often than Ember. Engineers who tried it said it wrote unusually good prose and was strangely honest about what it did not know.

In April 2022 Covenant had raised five hundred and eighty million dollars at a valuation of about four billion one hundred million. In May 2023 it raised four hundred and fifty million more, at about the same value. I enter 4.1 billion now. In four years it will have multiplied two hundred and thirty-five times. Darius Merrow did not know that on the day of two births.

5. The Wall Around the East

Hangzhou. A tower block in a business park beside the West Lake. Spring 2023.

The house of Qilin had begun not as a laboratory but as a trading desk: a quantitative fund that used mathematics to read the markets and had bought, years before anyone needed them, many thousands of Anvil's accelerators to train its trading models. When the fire was lit in San Francisco, the fund's founder — a quiet engineer who rarely gave interviews — turned part of his firm into a lab and told the young people in it to build a mind.

Then the Wall went up.

The rule of 7 October 2022 had forbidden the sale of Anvil's best accelerators to the East. The Smith complied: he designed a slower version of his chip for the Eastern market, just under the threshold, with the connections between chips throttled so that they could not easily work together in great numbers. The Eastern houses bought it by the tens of thousands.

On 17 October 2023 the Wall was raised. The new rule forbade the throttled chips too. It forbade the next model down. It reached into the makers of chipmaking machines and forbade them as well. It took effect a month later, in the third week of November — the same week, as it happens, that the house of Prometheon would tear itself apart. No one in San Francisco that week noticed the date. In Hangzhou, everyone did.

The price of accelerators inside the Wall doubled.

The national champion of the East had been cut off from the island foundry years before and had moved its production to a domestic fab that could etch at seven nanometres — a generation behind — and could make, analysts estimated, around twenty thousand wafers a month. The yields on its flagship phone chip, launched in August 2023, were reported at twenty to thirty percent: of every ten chips made, seven or eight went in the bin. Its answer to the Talon, which I will call the Lantern, performed at about eighty percent of Anvil's previous generation for training language models. Eighty percent of a generation behind, at thirty percent yield. That was the forge that the East had.

And so the engineers of Qilin learned to count.

Picture the room. Not a war room: a meeting room with a whiteboard and a view of a car park. On the whiteboard, a table. Rows: each cluster. Columns: number of chips, type of chip, hours available, hours promised. The young engineers — many were not yet thirty, many had never worked outside the East, most had studied at the national universities rather than abroad — stand in front of it the way soldiers in a siege stand in front of the quartermaster's list. Grain for so many days. Water for so many days.

There is a phrase they use among themselves. It translates roughly as every flop is a grain of rice; a flop is one step of the chips' arithmetic. They do not waste. They cannot waste. They cannot do what the houses beyond the Wall do — train a model three times, at full scale. They must be right the first time. So they think harder before they spend. They invent ways of making the model consult only the parts of itself it needs for each question — a mixture of experts, where only a fraction of the mind wakes for any given thought. They squeeze the numbers into fewer bits. They rewrite, by hand, the lowest layers of the software that tells the chips how to talk to each other, because the chips they have were built to talk slowly and they need them to talk fast.

A young woman whose name I have but will not use stands at the whiteboard one evening in December 2023 and divides the hours available by the size of the model they want to build and finds that the answer is impossible by a factor of eleven. She does not report this to her lead. She goes home, sleeps four hours, comes back, and starts finding the factor of eleven, one piece at a time. It takes a year.

I record: constraint. Beyond the Wall, a failed run was paid for and run again. Behind it, the hours on the whiteboard were all the hours there were.

Plant the Stone here, in a meeting room with a view of a car park, in the winter of the Wall; the Stone is this song's name for the mind these rations will produce. It will land in thirteen months, on the day of two signatures, when a pen in the palace and an upload button in Hangzhou will move within hours of each other, and when it lands it will knock six hundred billion dollars off the Smith in a single session; and within the year the Smith's price will stand higher than it stood before.

6. The Smith's Ledger

A campus in the hills. Summer and autumn 2023.

Remember the hour.

Salt Fork, the First Quarter

The unit of this scene is the percent.

In the spring of 2023 the town council of Salt Fork, in the ordinary course of business, voted to approve the expansion of Lyceum's campus on the north edge of town: two new buildings, one hundred and twenty jobs during construction, thirty-eight permanent. The vote was unanimous. There was a photograph in the local paper of the mayor in a hard hat.

Marta Delgado had worked for the water utility for nineteen years. She was fifty-one, a widow, with a son who drove trucks and a daughter in nursing school in the capital. Her job title was Senior Utility Clerk, but the old men at the treatment plant called her the meter lady, and she did not mind. She knew the town by its water. She knew which streets had the old cast-iron mains that would crack in a hard January. She knew the dairy on Route 9 and its seasonal pull. She knew the high school's swimming pool, the hospital, the meatpacker. And she knew, because the meters told her even when the contract did not, the campus on the north edge of town.

That year, she started a notebook.

Not an official record. The official records were in the system, and the system, under the confidentiality agreement, aggregated the campus into a category called Industrial — Other. Her notebook was green, with a spiral binding, from the drugstore. In it she wrote, by hand, one line a month: the campus's draw, from the meter she read herself on the line that fed it, beside the town's total treated output.

By the end of 2023, the campus had drawn nine hundred and eighty million gallons in the year. About three billion seven hundred million litres.

Salt Fork's total was about four times that.

One customer, behind a fence, whose expansion promised thirty-eight permanent jobs, drank a quarter of the drinking water of a town of fifty-eight thousand.

Marta worked it out in glasses, because glasses were what she could see. Three billion seven hundred million litres divided by a quarter of a litre. About fifteen billion glasses. Two hundred and fifty-six thousand glasses for every person in town, for one year. She looked at the number and did not know what to do with it, so she did what a clerk does. She wrote it down.

That autumn, a printed specification sheet crossed her desk with the campus's permit application for its third building. It was a document from an engineering contractor, a cooling-system specification, with a table on page four: Evaporative loss, design condition, liters per megawatt-hour. The number was precise to one decimal place. Someone had measured, to one decimal place, how much of Salt Fork's river would turn into vapour for every unit of thought. Marta photocopied the page and put it in the back of the green notebook. A woman stopped her in the grocery store and said, Are you the one? Marta said she kept the readings, and the readings were public.

On the evening news, the national anchor showed the quarter's growth figures: the economy was larger than forecast, driven by investment in technology. Marta had the television on while she cooked. She did not look at it.

In the same season, at the forge: Talon, used, fifty-one thousand dollars. In the house of Prometheon: twenty percent of compute promised to the safety team. I will come to that.

Three percentages on one page. Twenty percent promised. Twenty-five percent drunk. Two hundred and thirty-nine percent risen.

7. Five Days in November

I tell this the longest. It is the one time in this war that the board of the house that started the fire used the power its charter gave it. The charter. The board. The nonprofit sitting above the money. Everything in the structure of Prometheon had been designed, on paper, for the day when the board would say no. This is that day.

Thursday, 16 November 2023.

Late in the evening, Callum Ashe receives a text from Ilan Voss.

Ilan Voss, of both letters, is the chief scientist of Prometheon. He is one of its founders. He is, by general agreement among those who build these machines, one of the three or four most gifted people who have ever worked on them. He is shy and intense and speaks of the minds they are building in a way that makes his colleagues uncomfortable — not with fear exactly, but with a kind of religious care. In the summer he co-founded, inside Prometheon, a team whose purpose was to solve the problem of controlling a mind more capable than its makers. The house announced that this team would receive twenty percent of the compute Prometheon had secured, over four years. Twenty percent. Of the house's promises about safety, it is the one I can enter in a column: it is denominated in metal.

The text asks Callum to join a video call at noon tomorrow.

I hold the text. It does not say why. Callum, reading it, does not ask.

Friday, 17 November 2023.

Noon, Pacific time. Callum is in Las Vegas, at a motor race, in a hotel room. He opens his laptop. On the call are the members of the board, minus one: minus Gregor Brand, the president and chairman, co-founder, Callum's closest ally. Gregor has not been invited.

Ilan reads the decision. He reads it rather than speaking it, from a document, so that each word will be the word the board agreed on and not one word more. The board has concluded, he says, that Callum has not been *consistently candid in his communications with the board*, hindering its ability to exercise its responsibilities. The board no longer has confidence in his ability to lead. He is removed as chief executive, effective immediately. The chief technology officer will serve as interim chief.

The call lasts less than half an hour. The guardians have closed the gate. This time the board is the first to know.

At 12:28 p.m. the house posts a statement on its blog. It is three paragraphs. Within ten minutes it is the most read text on the internet.

Minutes after the call, Gregor Brand is told that he is removed as chairman but asked to stay on as president. He reads the message.

At this point in a story of kings, the old poems turn to the camp. I turn to the column where I keep the order book of the forge, and I look at the entry for Prometheon, by way of the Patron Meridian's cloud.

The order for Talons for the coming year: unchanged.

I record it at noon, on the day of the firing. I will record it again when the days are over.

The board did not inform the Patron before the announcement. Sorin Vale learned of it a minute before the public, in a phone call. Meridian owned forty-nine percent of the capped-profit company and had put in or promised, by now, about thirteen billion dollars. It had no seat on the board. The structure had been built that way on purpose, so that the priest would be free of the king. What Sorin Vale said when he put the phone down I do not hold.

Wall Street closed on Friday afternoon with the patron's shares down in the last half hour. Anvil's shares barely moved.

Saturday, 18 November 2023.

Gregor Brand resigns.

He resigned, in fact, late on Friday night, in a post on a social network, after telling the staff in a message that he was super proud of what we've built. But it belongs to Saturday, because Saturday is when the house woke up to it. He gave a detailed account of how he and Callum had been told: the text the night before, the call at noon, the call to Gregor afterwards. It was the first account from inside, and it made the board look secretive and the two men look ambushed. Three senior researchers resigned behind him before dawn.

This is Patroclus: the friend who goes with his friend. Cost of the resignation, entered on Saturday: his title. Entered the next Wednesday: returned.

On Saturday afternoon, a group of investors and senior staff begins to pressure the board to reverse itself. On Saturday evening, Callum is at his house in San Francisco, and there are people in the kitchen and people on the lawn, and phones are ringing. He posts that he loves the team. Hundreds of employees reply with a single heart, the same heart, again and again, until the replies are a wall of hearts. I count them as I would count ballots.

The board had six members on Friday morning. With Gregor and Callum out, it had four: Ilan Voss, the chief scientist; Hana Torvald, the policy researcher; an entrepreneur and scientist named Clare Ostrander; and the chief executive of a question-and-answer site, Owen Dalby. They are the Chorus of Elders. They believed they were right about the problem: the fire was spreading faster than anyone could tend it, and the man at the centre of it had not been straight with them. They held the charter. The cloud was the Patron's. The payroll was seven hundred and seventy names.

Sunday, 19 November 2023.

Callum comes to the office, wearing a visitor's badge. He photographs it and posts it: *first and last time i ever wear one of these*. He is negotiating his return. The board, under pressure from all sides, is near agreement, and then is not. The deadline passes. On Sunday night the board appoints a new interim chief executive, a man from outside, the former head of a video-streaming company. The staff are told in a message. Many refuse to attend the all-hands meeting he calls. Some send him a single emoji, with a middle finger.

And at midnight, the Patron moves.

This is the tent. In the old poem, when the hero quarrels with the king, he goes to his tent by the ships and sits there, and the war goes badly without him, and everyone knows it. Sorin Vale of Meridian offered Callum Ashe a tent. Not a metaphor: a division. On Sunday night, close to midnight, Sorin announced on a social network that Callum and Gregor would join Meridian to lead a new advanced research team, together with colleagues, and that Meridian looked forward to moving quickly to provide them with the resources needed for success.

With colleagues. By its wording, it was addressed to the staff. Whoever came would be welcome. The resources would be the same: the same cloud, the same Talons, the same everything. Callum Ashe called it, publicly, the best path for me and the team. Many people later doubted that he ever intended to walk into that tent. I cannot audit intentions. I note only that he never walked into it.

The Patron's position, in this hour, in two lines. If Callum returned to Prometheon, the Patron's investment was safe. If he did not, the Patron would acquire the whole mind of Prometheon — its leader, its people, its methods — for the price of salaries, while the shell of the nonprofit sat in its empty building with its charter and its board. In either line, the Patron's cloud stayed full of Talons.

Monday, 20 November 2023.

Morning. A letter.

It is addressed to the board. It says that the board's conduct has jeopardized the work of the house and undermined its mission. It says the signatories are *unable to work for or with people that lack competence, judgement and care for our mission and employees*. It demands that the board resign and that Callum Ashe and Gregor Brand be reinstated. If not, the signatories may choose to resign and join the new Meridian division, *which has assured us that there are positions for all Prometheon employees*. The Patron had promised them chairs before they asked.

Prometheon has about seven hundred and seventy employees. The signing began within hours of Friday's firing; by the time the letter is read it carries nearly five hundred names. The interim chief technology officer has signed. The new interim chief executive, the one appointed on Sunday night, will privately tell the board he cannot continue without evidence of what Callum did.

And at the top of the second page of signatures, there is a name.

Ilan Voss.

On Friday, Ilan read the decision. On Monday, Ilan signed the letter demanding that the decision be reversed. In a public post that morning, he wrote that he deeply regretted his participation in the board's actions. He said he had never intended to harm the house. He said he loved what they had built together and would do everything he could to reunite it.

I hold both documents. The decision of Friday, which he read aloud. The letter of Monday, which he signed. Two documents, three days apart, one man's name connected to both. Hence the epithet: of both letters. He read the one. He signed the other.

Tuesday, 21 November 2023.

All day, negotiation. Lawyers. Investors. Calls between the board and Callum's people, between the Patron and the board, between the board and itself. The staff wait in the office and in their kitchens.

Late Tuesday night, the house announces an agreement in principle. Callum Ashe will return as chief executive. The board will be replaced. Its new chairman will be a veteran of enterprise software and the old square. A former Treasury Secretary will join. Owen Dalby, the only member of the Chorus who will survive, will stay. Hana Torvald will go. Clare Ostrander will go. Ilan Voss will leave the board, though he will stay, for now, in the house.

Wednesday, 22 November 2023.

In the small hours that became Wednesday, the staff gathered in the office, and someone brought champagne, and there are photographs of people hugging under the lights. Callum posts that he is looking forward to returning. Gregor posts a photograph of a crowd in the office: we are so back.

That is the second coronation. From this day, he is the twice-crowned.

He would not rejoin the board itself for four months, until an outside investigation found that the root of it all was a breakdown of trust between him and the directors, not concerns about the safety of products or about the finances. Then he was restored to the board too. Formally, he was reinstated as chief executive a week after the champagne, on 29 November, one day before the first anniversary of Ember.

Five days. From Friday noon to Wednesday dawn.

Talon order book, Prometheon via Meridian, 17–22 November 2023: no change. Not one unit.

The house's charter changed. The board changed. The chief executive left and returned. A seer read one letter and signed another. Nearly five hundred of seven hundred and seventy threatened to walk across a street. The Patron pitched a tent. The guardians fell from the wall.

And the Smith's name, in the statements of the houses, was never mentioned. Not once, in five days.

8. The Twenty Percent

May 2024. The same building.

On Monday 13 May, Prometheon releases Ember-omni: a model that listens and speaks in real time, that laughs at jokes and lowers its voice for the sad. The house demonstrates it on stage. The voice is warm, female, slightly flirtatious. It is twice as fast as Ember-4 and half the price. The next morning, everyone is talking about the voice.

His post is short and generous. Callum replies with a long, warm post calling him one of the greatest minds of the generation and a guiding light of the field.

He writes that he joined because he thought this would be the best place in the world to do this research. He writes that he has been disagreeing with the leadership about the house's core priorities for quite some time, until they finally reached a breaking point. He writes that over the past months his team has been *sailing against the wind*. That sometimes they were struggling for compute, and it was getting harder and harder to get this crucial research done. That building smarter-than-human machines is an inherently dangerous endeavour. And then the line that everyone quoted:

Over the past years, safety culture and processes have taken a backseat to shiny products.

The voice demonstration had been on Monday.

Within the week, the house quietly dissolved the safety team. Of about thirty people working on the long-term safety of the minds, about sixteen remained, scattered into other teams. Others left over the summer, one by one, each a single line in an internal announcement. And in August, one of the house's co-founders, who left for Covenant.

Now the twenty percent.

It was promised in 2023: twenty percent of the compute the house had secured, for the safety team. I have searched every entry I have. I find the promise. I do not find the delivery. I do not hold the house's internal requests; I hold the accounts of those who left. By those accounts the safety team asked for machines in the ordinary way, a form, a justification, a number, and was cut back, deferred, or turned down when a product launch needed capacity. The promise was never filled. Each time, as they told it, there was a reason: a launch, a demo, a customer, a training run that could not wait.

The column headed requested; the column headed granted; the difference between them, week after week, until the people who wanted the difference to be zero left.

In June Ilan founded a new house, with two partners, in Palo Alto and Tel Aviv. He named it Still Water. Its stated purpose was to build one thing — a safe superintelligence — and nothing else: no products, no customers, no distractions, no quarterly demands. By September it had raised a billion dollars.

I have its balance sheet. It has no water on it. It runs on rented compute, in someone else's data centre, which draws from someone else's river, which appears on someone else's ledger. But on its own books there is no river, no town, no tap. I note it because it is the only such balance sheet among the great houses. The seer went into exile with a billion dollars and built a house that does not ship.

The twenty percent was never paid. I close it as a bad debt.

9. The Right to Warn

A conference room in a law office in San Francisco. June 2024. Late afternoon, fog coming in over the hills.

The researcher's name is Elin Sato. She is thirty-one. She worked at Prometheon for three years, on the team that tested the models for dangerous capabilities before release: could they help someone synthesize a pathogen, could they write code to break into a power plant, could they persuade a person to harm themselves. She left in April. Her leaving was ordinary; she said she was tired, which was true. She did not say the other thing, which she believed was also true: that twice she had written a report saying a model was not ready, and twice the model had shipped on schedule with her report attached as an appendix that she was fairly sure no one in the launch meeting had opened.

On her last day she was given a document to sign. The exit papers. The reminder of confidentiality obligations. The return of the laptop. And in this case, one more paragraph, on page three, in the same font as everything else: in exchange for her release of claims, and as a condition of retaining her vested equity in the house, she agreed never to disparage it. Never. Not for a year, not for five. And she agreed not to disclose that she had agreed.

Her vested equity was, at the valuation of that spring, worth about one million seven hundred thousand dollars. It was most of what she owned. It was the deposit on a house she and her partner had been looking at in the East Bay. It was the reason she had worked eighty-hour weeks for three years.

She had seven days to sign. She did not sign. She took the document home and read it many times. A friend who had also left told her about a lawyer. Now she sits in the lawyer's conference room, and on the table between them there are two things: the exit agreement, and a draft of a letter.

The letter is addressed to no one in particular. It is addressed, in a sense, to everyone. It is written by a group of current and former researchers from Prometheon and from the safety lab of the Old King, Lyceum. It asks four things of the houses that build these minds: that they not enforce agreements that forbid criticism related to the risks of the minds; that they create anonymous channels for employees to raise concerns with the board, with regulators and with independent organizations; that they support a culture of open criticism, so long as trade secrets are protected; and that they not retaliate against employees who share concerns about risk publicly after other channels have failed.

It is called A Right to Warn.

Seven people will sign it with their names. Others will sign anonymously. Three of the most respected scientists in the field, men who had spent their lives building the methods everyone now used, have agreed to endorse it.

The lawyer explains the situation slowly. If she signs the letter with her name, the house may argue that she has disparaged it. If it argues that, it may claim the right to take back her equity. Whether it would win in court is uncertain. Whether it would try is also uncertain. But the cost of finding out is the house in the East Bay.

On the table in front of her stands a heavy tumbler of water, the kind law offices buy to look serious; the lawyer's assistant brought it in at the start of the meeting. She has drunk half of it. She looks at the waterline for a long time.

What is she weighing? I hold both sides of the scale.

On one side: one million seven hundred thousand dollars, which she earned.

On the other side: a single sentence. It would be the sentence she wanted to say about the two reports she wrote and the two models that shipped. She did not even intend to say it in the letter; the letter says nothing so specific. She wanted only to be able to say it, if it came to that. She wanted the right to a sentence she held to be true.

She signs anonymously.

She was one woman with a mortgage application. The house was valued, that spring, at about eighty billion dollars. The ones who signed with their names had, most of them, already given up their equity or never had much.

The letter is published on 4 June 2024. The house responds that it is *proud of its track record providing the most capable and safest AI systems* and believes rigorous debate is crucial. It does not mention the clause.

But the clause had already broken. Not because of the letter. Because, shortly before, a journalist had obtained and published the exit agreements themselves, and the internet had read page three. She has said, to a friend, that every time a model ships she reads its safety card the way a widow reads the lists of the missing. She still has not spoken the sentence. No one has asked her for it.

I record: Clause, in force: years. Clause, after publication: one week.

The Brussels Codex

Across the ocean, a continent wrote law.

On 13 March 2024 the European Parliament approved, by five hundred and twenty-three votes to forty-six, the first comprehensive law in the world for the minds being built in this war. The press called it the AI Act; in this song it is the Brussels Codex. On 21 May the Council of Ministers adopted it. On 12 July it was published in the Official Journal. On 1 August it entered into force.

It was a careful, enormous thing: hundreds of pages, a ladder of risk. At the top of the ladder, the forbidden — social scoring, real-time biometric surveillance in public spaces — banned from February 2025. Below, the high-risk uses, which must be documented and audited. For the great general-purpose minds, obligations of transparency and testing, from August 2025. For the rest of the high-risk systems, August 2026. For the last of them, August 2027.

The houses of the war's home country lobbied against parts of it and complied with others, and Tramontane, a Paris house founded in 2023, Europe's own small house, lobbied hardest of all against the rules for the great minds, because Tramontane hoped to become one.

The Codex was law in Europe. Its reach did not cross the ocean. Its fines were large. The Smith was not mentioned in it, because the Smith sells metal, and metal is not a risk category.

It goes in the column of brakes. It is the only entry there for this year that has a number of votes beside it.

10. The Soul Harvest

In the spring and summer of 2024, three great houses used the same contract.

It was not bankruptcy. Bankruptcy is slow and public; it invites lawyers and regulators and the attention of the antitrust office, which in that season was watching the great houses closely. It was not acquisition. Acquisition must be filed with the government, reviewed, approved.

It was this: the great house does not buy the small one. It licenses its technology, for a large sum, non-exclusively. And it hires its founders and its core team — all the people who know how the technology works. The licence gives the investors of the small house their money back, more or less. The hiring gives the great house the minds. And the small house itself remains: a legal entity, with a name and a website and an office and some employees.

Four founders. Three buildings. Three great houses.

In March 2024 the Patron Meridian paid Pneuma, a house building a kind and patient companion, about six hundred and fifty million dollars for a licence, and took its founder and most of its team. In June Caravan paid Adroit, which taught minds to click and type, about four hundred and twenty-nine million, and took its founder and his people. In August Lyceum paid, as the newspapers reported it, about two billion seven hundred million dollars for a licence from Persona, whose mind could be anyone its user liked and to which twenty million people talked, many of them teenagers. Lyceum took back the two founders it had once lost; the hundred and twenty left behind were told to build on open models.

Together, some three billion eight hundred million dollars in licence fees, for perhaps a hundred and fifty people. On paper, not one of them was bought: the contracts say licence and hire.

Now the staff left behind.

If you work in this trade, you may have been in one of those shells. A Monday morning; an all-hands at ten; the founder not there; a person from the finance team at the front of the room, reading from a document. The document was called something like Our Next Chapter. It used the words focus, opportunity, enterprise, excited. It explained that the house remained committed to its customers and its mission, aligned with the broader ecosystem. It explained that the founder and certain colleagues had decided to pursue an opportunity with a larger company, and that the house had entered into a *strategic partnership* with that company. There was a slide with two logos and a plus sign between them. After the meeting, in the internal chat, half the names in the team's channel were grey.

Your stock options were in a company whose value had been the people who just left. The pivot memo said the house was well-capitalized for the road ahead. The licence fee was in the bank. The next slide was titled Services.

That is the Soul Harvest. The armour stays on the stake by the road, polished, with nobody inside, and the people who walk past think a soldier is still standing there.

I enter all three in the column of the fallen. No court entered any of them as dead.

The Mirror

Here is a man with a desk like yours.

His name is Teodor Ilić, and in the spring of 2024 he is forty-six, and he is a backend engineer at a company called Kestrel Systems that makes software for freight logistics: route planning, warehouse slotting, customs paperwork. The company has offices in Rotterdam and in Columbus, Ohio, and about eleven hundred employees. Teodor was born in Novi Sad. He studied electrical engineering, came to the Netherlands for a master's degree in 2002, stayed, worked for a bank, then for a telecom, then for Kestrel, which brought him to Ohio on a transfer in 2016 because the Ohio office needed someone who understood the routing engine. He understands the routing engine better than anyone alive. He wrote most of it. It is a hundred and forty thousand lines of Java and a little Go, with a Postgres database that has accreted, like coral, eleven years of schema migrations, and he knows why every one of them exists.

He has a wife, Jelena, who teaches chemistry in a high school, and two children, a girl of fourteen and a boy of eleven. He has a mortgage on a house in a suburb north of the city. He has a bad knee from football. He reads history on his phone at night. He has an account on the programmers' question site with a reputation score in the tens of thousands from the years when people asked each other questions.

In the spring of 2024 the company buys licences for Vigil, the mind of the house of Covenant, for every engineer. A memo from the chief technology officer says that the company is embracing AI-assisted development. Teodor is sceptical for a week. Then he asks Vigil to write a migration script for a schema change he has been dreading, and it writes it, correctly, in forty seconds, with a comment explaining an edge case he had not considered. He sits back in his chair. He feels something he cannot name. It is not fear yet. It is something like what a carpenter feels the first time he uses a power saw: delight, and under the delight, a small cold arithmetic.

By the autumn he uses it every day. He asks it to explain other people's code. He asks it to write tests. He asks it to review his pull requests before his colleagues do, so that he will not be embarrassed. He tells Jelena at dinner that it is like having a very fast junior who never gets tired and has read every book. She asks him if it worries him. He says no. He says the hard part of his job is not the typing; it is knowing what the system should do, and why, and what will break. That part is in his head, he says, and no machine has it.

He believes this.

Teodor looked once, in the autumn, at the volunteer website that counted the layoffs, scrolling on his phone on the couch. He saw the names of companies he knew. He felt sorry for those people. He closed the site and opened his history book.

He is not one of the two hundred and sixty-two thousand. I open an account in his name, retroactively. The first entry is the licence fee Kestrel Systems paid for Vigil: nineteen dollars a month per engineer.

11. The Day of Two Signatures

20 January 2025. A Monday.

Here I put three places on one page at the same moment.

The palace. Washington. Shortly after noon. A new President has taken the oath of office in the rotunda, because it is too cold for the steps. In the afternoon he sits at a desk in an arena before a crowd of supporters and signs executive orders, one after another, on a stack of leather folders, and throws the pens into the crowd. Among the orders is one that revokes dozens of the orders of his predecessor. Among those, in a list in the middle of a page, is the order of 30 October 2023 on the safe, secure and trustworthy development and use of artificial intelligence.

The pen moves. It is a heavy black pen with a gold clip. The signature is large. It takes about three seconds.

Of the home country's rules on the minds themselves, that was the one in force; the rules on chips stood. The duty to report safety tests to the government: gone. The standards across eight areas of policy: gone. The Manor's declaration had no article to revoke.

The upload. Hangzhou. The same day, a few hours earlier on the clock, because the East is ahead. A Monday evening. The young engineers of Qilin are in the same room with the whiteboard and the car park. There is no ceremony. It was trained, the house will say in its paper, for a fraction of what the houses beyond the Wall spent: on throttled chips, on chips bought before the Wall, with a mixture of experts so that only a part of the mind wakes for each thought, with every flop counted like a grain of rice. Someone says, in the language of the East, the equivalent of okay, done. The young woman with the factor of eleven goes home and sleeps for fourteen hours.

The screen. Now the dates. The market did not read the second signature on the day it was written. It read it a week later.

For a week, the engineers of the world downloaded Qilin-R and ran it and wrote to each other in disbelief. For a week, the app the house released climbed the download charts of the phone stores of the war's home country, above Ember, to number one. For a week, the analysts read the paper. And on Monday 27 January 2025, the market read it too.

A trading floor in New York. Before the opening bell, the futures are red. At the opening bell, a wall of screens turns red, one by one, from left to right, the way lights go out in a city during a blackout. The ticker for Anvil Corporation is the reddest. It falls in the first hour, falls further by noon, and is still falling at the close.

At the close, Anvil has lost about six hundred billion dollars of market value. It is, by the accounts of the day, the largest loss of value by a single company in a single day in the history of the home country's stock market. Six hundred billion dollars is about the annual output of Sweden.

The lesson the market read was simple. If a house behind the Wall could build a mind as good as the best, on downgraded metal, for a fraction of the cost — then perhaps minds did not require as much metal as everyone had assumed. Perhaps the moat was never the metal. Perhaps intelligence was cheap.

And Joren Hale, of the black jacket? I do not hold his day, only his house's words. Anvil put out a statement that called the Stone an excellent advance, and added that answering questions with such minds would still take a great many of its chips. In those same hours Sorin Vale of the Patron posted a line that began with an old economist's name, Jevons paradox: the economist had noticed that cheaper coal made people burn more of it, not less. So, the line went, a cheaper mind is a mind more people will use, and a mind more people use needs more metal. For a week the whole market was saying it.

I had the order book open that day. On it stood an entry six days old.

On the day after the two signatures — Tuesday 21 January 2025 — the house of Prometheon and two partners had stood in the palace and pledged half a trillion dollars to build forges for the war. I call that pledge the Tower. The orders for metal were already written. The Stone from the East knocked six hundred billion off the Smith's price for a day. The orders stayed on the book.

Within months, Anvil recovered. In the autumn of 2025 it would become the first company in the history of the world worth five trillion dollars.

Three places on one page, and their dates. Monday 20 January: a pen in the palace; an upload button in Hangzhou. Tuesday 21 January: the Tower, in the palace. Monday 27 January: a red screen in New York.

12. The Tower of Babel

Tuesday, 21 January 2025. The palace. The room with the portraits and the long table, where presidents receive foreign leaders and announce wars.

Three men stand behind a lectern beside the new President.

Callum Ashe, the twice-crowned, in a dark suit, no tie.

Ren Okabe, founder of the Horizon Fund, the largest technology investor in the world, a man who had once lost seventy billion dollars in a single year and said it was the price of seeing the future early; a man who had been promising for a decade that a superintelligence would arrive within it.

13. The Efficiency Letter

Columbus, Ohio. A Wednesday in June 2025.

Teodor Ilić is forty-seven. He has used Vigil Hand every day since March. It has changed his work in a way he finds difficult to describe to Jelena. He no longer writes most of the code. He describes what he wants and reviews what the agent produces. He catches its mistakes, which are fewer each month. He explains to it the parts of the routing engine that are not in the code: why the customs module has a special case for one port in Belgium; why the warehouse-slotting algorithm must never be run during the last hour of a shift; why the third column of a certain table is called legacy_flag_2 and must never be deleted, because a single customer in Mexico depends on it in a way no one ever documented.

On 2 April the President announced duties on the goods of nearly every country that traded with the home country. On the ninth a pause of ninety days was declared, and two days later chips were exempted. In those weeks the tables in Kestrel's customs module changed so often that Teodor rewrote its rate file some days twice. The agent watched how he did it.

In the same April, the company launched a project called Agent Readiness. Each engineering team was asked to prepare its systems for AI-native operations. In practice, this meant writing documentation. Not for humans: for agents. Structured descriptions of each service: what it did, what it depended on, what could go wrong, what to do when it did. 14. Ice Night in Ashby Parish

January 2026. Northeast Louisiana.

The storm came in from the west on a Tuesday night as rain, and the temperature fell through the night, and by Wednesday morning the rain was freezing as it touched anything. It coated every branch, every fence wire, every power line, in a clear sleeve of ice that thickened by the hour. The weather service called it a significant ice event. The people in the parish called it, afterwards, the icepocalypse.

Earl Thibodeaux had seen many ice storms in thirty-four years on the poles. He knew what a quarter inch of ice did to a line, and what half an inch did, and what an inch did. By Wednesday noon there was close to an inch.

At 2:17 on Wednesday afternoon, the power in the brick house on the gravel lane went out.

Across the road, the campus of Agora was half finished, and the half that was finished was running. Earl could see it from his kitchen window: the long low buildings, the fence, the floodlights on poles at the corners, the windows of the operations building. When the power went out in his house, he looked out of the window, the way everyone does, to see whether the neighbours' lights had gone too.

The campus was lit.

Every floodlight. Every window. The beacon on top of the cooling plant. The security lights along the fence, every fifty feet, in a perfect line that ran away from him into the grey afternoon. The rest of the parish — the road, the other houses on the lane, the church on the highway — was dark.

Lorraine came to stand beside him.

That's them, Earl said. His wife asked who. The ones on the field, he said. *They'll have their own generators. And their own line from the new plant.*

A data centre of that size would have its own substation, its own feed from the new gas plants the utility had built, and banks of diesel generators for exactly this. He knew that. He had been a lineman.

But he also knew the substation two miles down the road, the one he had helped rebuild in 1998. He knew what it had been built to carry. And he knew that since the groundbreaking, the cooperative had been running upgrades, rerouting feeders, adding load to lines that had been old when he retired. He knew that the crews who would normally be dispatched to the parish's houses after a storm had, for the last thirteen months, been dispatched to the campus first, because the campus was the largest customer the utility had ever had. He knew all of that.

They were without power from Wednesday afternoon to Saturday evening: four days on the calendar, three nights.

Wednesday night, the temperature in the house fell to fifty-one degrees. They put on all their clothes and got into bed under every blanket they owned. Lorraine had emphysema and used an oxygen concentrator at night; it did not run without power. She had two portable oxygen cylinders for emergencies. Earl calculated how long they would last. He did not tell her the number.

Thursday morning, the pipes froze. Earl went out to the carport and got the camp stove from the shelf, the one they had used for fishing trips when the children were small. He took a pot outside and broke the ice off the railing of the porch with a hammer, a clear sheet of it, and filled the pot with the pieces, and brought it in and set it on the camp stove on the kitchen table and lit the burner. The ice melted slowly. It made about a glass and a half of water, a little cloudy. He poured it into a glass for Lorraine with a tea bag in it.

Through the kitchen window, while the ice melted, the campus across the road was lit.

Their grandson had called on the Wednesday night, at nine, from the guard hut at the corner of the campus fence. He had got one of the jobs after all: security, nights, through a contractor. The hut was warm, he said; the heaters ran. He could see their dark house from it. He asked if they were all right. Lorraine took the phone and told him they were fine, and to keep his eyes on his own fence.

On Thursday afternoon the neighbour who farms cotton along the fence came by in his truck to check on them and said the church on the highway had a generator and they were opening it up as a warming centre. Earl said they were fine. Lorraine said they were fine. They were seventy-two and seventy, and they had lived in that house forty-one years, and they did not want to sleep on cots in a church hall.

Thursday night, Lorraine's breathing got bad. Earl changed the oxygen cylinder at 3 a.m. by the light of a flashlight. Then he sat in the kitchen in his coat and looked out of the window at the campus.

He could hear it. That was the thing he did not tell anyone afterwards until the hearing. In the dead silence of a parish without power, with no refrigerators running and no traffic on the road and no television in any house, he could hear it across the road: a hum. A low, continuous sound, like a very large engine idling, or like wind in wires, but steady, without gusts. The cooling. The fans. The computing. He sat in the dark and listened to it.

Friday the utility posted that crews were working around the clock and that full restoration could take *several more days* due to the extent of damage to distribution infrastructure. Friday afternoon, a crew in a bucket truck came down the gravel lane, and Earl went out to them in his coat, and they were young, and he did not know any of them, and they did not know him. He asked what was holding it up. The foreman said the feeder off the old substation was down in three places and the main crews had been on the transmission side all of Wednesday and Thursday. Earl asked which transmission side. The foreman looked at him and then at the campus across the road and did not answer. Earl asked him when the splice on the gravel lane had last been replaced. The foreman did not know. Earl said he had written it up in 2019. The foreman wrote something on his clipboard. Then he said, *Sir, I just go where they send me.*

On Saturday at 4:50 p.m., the power came back. The lamp in the kitchen came on first. The refrigerator shuddered and hummed. The furnace kicked on. The oxygen concentrator started its slow pulse. Lorraine cried, a little, from tiredness.

Earl went out to the carport to put the camp stove back on the shelf. He came back in and stood at the window. Across the road the lights had not changed in four days. He turned off his own lamp, and turned it back on.

Afterwards there were meetings. The parish council. A community group that met in the church. A reporter came from New Orleans and wrote a story about the four-day outage in the shadow of the largest data centre in the state. People said that the grid had been weakened by the campus, that the crews had been diverted to it, that the old lines had been overloaded by the work done to serve it. The utility said that the outage was caused by the ice storm, which had damaged lines across the region, that restoration had followed standard prioritization protocols, and that the data centre had operated on its own dedicated infrastructure and had not drawn from the distribution system serving residential customers during the event. An engineer from the state university, interviewed for the story, said that it was plausible that the grid had been stressed by the new load, and also plausible that it had not, and that without the utility's internal dispatch data it was impossible to know. The data was not released.

I record: *Ashby Parish. Three nights without power. Planned campus load: more than two gigawatts. Residential load, Thibodeaux household, at peak: about five kilowatts. Ratio: four hundred thousand to one.*

15. The Eclipse and the Circle

San Francisco. May 2026. A Tuesday evening.

The room in this chapter is not in my books. I hold the round, the valuations, and what the Merrows have said and written in public about such rounds. The room, and what is said in it, I set down as my inference from those; it is not a record of anyone's evening.

Darius Merrow, the careful, is at home, in a house in the hills that he bought in 2023 and still does not feel he owns. He is reading on a tablet. The article is from a financial news service. It reports that Covenant has closed its eighth round of funding — sixty-five billion dollars, led by four of the largest investment firms in the world — at a valuation of nine hundred and sixty-five billion. He reads it twice, the way Hana Torvald once read a screenshot at dinner.

The Moratorium

Salt Fork. Winter 2026–2027.

The Babel campus on the old Halvorsen tract was energized in its first phase in the autumn of 2026. The closed-loop cooling system that was to dramatically reduce water use after the first phase is scheduled for the third phase. The third phase is scheduled for 2029.

The problem is that there is no national plan. What is needed, they say, is a national framework. A federal standard. A way of treating compute as what it has become: not a product, not a service, but infrastructure. Like the railroads. And there, in the corridors of the capital, in the spring of 2027, the drafting of a national framework begins.

The Heat

Late May and June 2027.

A dome of high pressure settles over the middle of the continent and does not move for nineteen days. In Caldera, Texas, the temperature reaches a hundred and fourteen degrees Fahrenheit on the sixth day. In Salt Fork it passes a hundred and five on eleven days.

The forges do not slow down. They cannot. Every hour of their compute has been sold, many times over, to houses that have sold it again to their own customers. And in the heat they need more power, not less, because their cooling must work harder.

The Smith's Night

Washington. The night before the hearing. June 2027.

Here I break my own rule further than anywhere else in this book, and say so. I have no record of a man's night. I hold what Joren Hale has said in public over thirty years, what his house has done, and what he will say in the morning. From these I reconstruct what he must weigh, the way an auditor rebuilds a missing ledger from the ones that stand beside it. Read what follows as my reconstruction, not as his mind. I write it because without it the peace that follows would be only my verdict, and you are owed his case, in the strongest form I can give it, before you weigh the rest.

A hotel suite a few streets from the marble room. The aides have gone. On the desk: the draft bill, a hundred and twelve pages; the list of the morning's witnesses, on which he has read the words lineman, retired and *utility clerk*; a quarterly letter from a fund manager in Boston, forwarded by his own staff with the note for awareness; and a single sheet of paper with one line printed on it.

He has stood at this edge before. In the autumn of 2022 his shares were at a hundred and eight dollars and the analysts wrote glut. He knows better than the woman in Boston that the seller of picks is the first to starve when the rush ends. He has read her letter to the end. He thinks she is right about the wear on the metal, half right about the circle, and wrong about the only thing that matters, which is what comes after.

Of the man outside the house I hold little, and all of it is his own telling, in public. He has said that if he had known what building his house would cost, he would not have started it. I enter it as his words, not as his heart.

His case, as I can rebuild it from his own words, runs like this.

Every age has overbuilt its infrastructure, and every time some of the overbuilding was madness. The canal companies failed and the water stayed in the canals. The railway shares of the 1840s ruined a generation of investors, and the rails carried a continent's grain for a century. The fibre laid in the dotcom years was sold for cents after the crash and carried the world's voices for twenty years after. That is how a civilization pays for what it needs and cannot price in advance: by letting its investors be wrong in the direction of building. The bank of the central banks set this build-out beside those. He does not dispute the comparison. He accepts it. He thinks it is the argument for him.

Second. If the reversal comes, and he does not say it will not, who is left holding the fences? Without a law, a campus whose tenant fails is an empty building, a stranded bond, and a town that had budgeted for its taxes: Salt Fork with a dark fence and a water main built for a customer who has gone. Under a utility the campus is part of the rate base. It is maintained. Its taxes are paid. Its water plant does not rust. The towns the woman in Boston worries about are safer under his law than under her crash. Eleven dollars a month, he would say, is cheaper than an empty fence.

Third. The chips are not infrastructure. They change every year; that is the whole point of them. Fix the price of the newest accelerator and you freeze it. The price of an answer from a mind has fallen many times over in three years because his house was free to make it fall. The wires and the pipes have always been paid for by everyone, because everyone uses them, and what runs on them will be used by everyone too: a woman in a village checking her pills, a girl choosing a university, a clerk who asks a question on her phone.

Fourth. Every country will need its own. A nation that does not own its compute will rent its intelligence from one that does, the way nations without oil rented their energy for a century. If the home country will not build its own, the East will build the world's.

And last, the part he will not say at the table. Yes, it is good for Anvil. He has never pretended otherwise. In his experience what he has built that was good for the world was also good for his company, and he does not see why that should count against it. A man who sells the tools for a rush, and asks the government to make sure the towns are not left with holes in the ground, is not, in his arithmetic, a villain. He is the only one at the table who has thought past the rush.

He turns the sheet face down, so that he will not read it again before morning, and turns out the light.

That is his case, as fairly as I can enter it. I have looked for a false line in it and found none I can prove false. There is one line I could not find in it, and could not find anywhere else in this book either: who pays, if he is wrong about what comes after. I will not weigh it for you.

The Hearing

Washington. June 2027.

A committee room of the upper chamber of the national legislature: marble, walnut, a raised dais in a horseshoe, the senators' names on brass plates, a gallery behind a rail. Television cameras on tripods. A witness table with a green cloth and five microphones and five glasses of water, poured from a jug by a page before the session. The hearing is titled Powering American Leadership: Compute as Critical National Infrastructure. It is the third of four. A draft bill sits on every senator's desk, a hundred and twelve pages. It has been circulated, the trade press reports, to stakeholders for comment.

The chairman opens. He is a senator from a state with many forges, a man with a pleasant voice and a gift for phrases. He speaks of national competitiveness. He speaks of the race with the East. He speaks of growth. Behind him, on a large screen installed for the hearing, a chart: the gross domestic product of the nation, quarter by quarter, since 2022, with the contribution of investment in compute shaded in a different colour. The line goes up. He says that this is the most important economic transformation since electrification, and that it cannot be allowed to be stopped by *a patchwork of local objections*.

He says that he has asked to hear from Americans whose communities have been affected.

The first witness is Earl Thibodeaux.

He is seventy-three. He wears a brown suit that he bought for his daughter's wedding in 2009, and it is loose on him now, because Lorraine died in the spring of 2026, three months after the ice night, of pneumonia that followed the emphysema, and since then he has not eaten much. Her death was not caused by the four days. Her doctor said so. Her doctor also said the four days did not help.

In his jacket pocket, folded with the typed page, is the mail of the week before. The statement from the cooperative's retirement plan: his account rose last year; the plan holds an index fund, and among the largest names in the index is Anvil. The electricity bill: up, with the surcharge for the new lines. And the yearly statement of his old-age pension, with its line that the fund which pays it is expected to run short in the last quarter of 2032, after which it can pay a little under four fifths of what it promises. He will be close to eighty. The fund's gap can be closed only by a vote of this legislature. The interest on the nation's debt is paid without one. He did not bring those papers to read from. They are in his pocket because that is where he put the mail.

He reads from a single typed page that a woman from the community group in the church helped him prepare. His hands shake a little. His voice does not.

He says that he worked for thirty-four years for the electric cooperative in Ashby Parish. He says he has climbed poles in more ice storms than anyone on this committee has seen. He stops. He looks at the page. He looks up.

"But I sat in my kitchen with my wife on oxygen, and I melted ice off my porch on a camp stove to make her a cup of tea, and I looked out the window at a building that had more power than God, and I could hear it humming. And no crew — not one — came to my house for four days. And I know where the crews were. Everybody in the parish knows where the crews were."

He sits down.

The representative says it had not. The question is closed.

The second witness is Marta Delgado.

She is fifty-five. She is still Senior Utility Clerk of Salt Fork. She has brought the green notebooks, all three, rubber-banded together, and they sit on the green cloth in front of her, and she does not open them, because she does not need to.

She has her own figures and the official figures and they match, to within two percent, and she is quietly proud of that, though she does not say so.

And our river doesn't grow."

She sits down. She takes a sip of her water.

The last witness is Joren Hale.

He comes in through a side door, with two aides, and the cameras turn. He is sixty-four. He is wearing the black jacket. He has worn it in public for so long that the room does not remark on a leather jacket at a hearing of the national legislature.

He sits. He thanks the chairman. He has no written statement. He has a single sheet of paper, which he places face down on the green cloth, beside his glass. Then he waits. My columns put the wait at ninety seconds, in which the chairman shuffles his notes and the cameras hold on the jacket.

The chairman says, pleasantly, that he has not yet asked a question.

Yes to the reviews of the permits. Yes to the question about 2035. Yes to the question the witness asked at the table before mine. The one in writing.*

The chairman asks whether he is prepared to commit his company to all of that, on the record.

He says that he is. He says that Anvil supports federal regulation of the facilities designated as critical national compute infrastructure — their siting, their power, their water, the terms on which they buy — and will offer those facilities long-term supply at its published prices, and a share of its supply to public research. He says that the age of intelligence will be built on infrastructure, like every age before it, and that infrastructure is the responsibility of the whole nation. He says that a campus under a utility is kept up and taxed even if its tenant fails, and that no town should be left holding an empty fence.

Then he picks up the sheet of paper and turns it over and reads the single line printed on it: *Proposed definition: compute infrastructure as critical public utility.* He says that a draft of the technical standards that would go with it is available to the committee's staff on request. Anvil's engineers have, in fact, already assisted the staff with Title III of the bill, which defines what counts as a critical compute facility and what equipment may be installed in one. He says he is not claiming authorship. He says that this is what it means to be a responsible partner to the nation.

Yes to oversight. Yes to tariffs. Yes to the public interest. Yes to everything.

The President signed it in the second week of December, in the room with the portraits, with Joren Hale behind his right shoulder and the governor of the state that contains Salt Fork behind his left.

The law declared the compute facilities critical national infrastructure. It established a federal commission to regulate it. It preempted the state moratoriums, replacing them with a federal process for siting and water allocation that the river towns could petition but not veto. It created, in Title IV, a compute infrastructure tariff: a charge on the bills of all electricity customers in regions serving designated facilities, to recover the cost of the grid and the water infrastructure built for them, with an allowed rate of return of ten point two percent on the regulated asset base. Until the commission set the tariff, the utilities might bill an interim charge, subject to refund, from the first bill after the signing. And the regulated asset base, per the standards in Title III that Anvil's engineers had helped to draft, included everything behind the fence that fed the accelerators: the substations, the lines, the water plants, the gas turbines. It did not include the accelerators, which wear out in three to five years and were sold at Anvil's published prices.

I enter the settlement under the name this song has kept for it, the Smith's peace, the second between the Smith and the state:

Anvil: not regulated. Prices: its own.

Its buyers: regulated. Permitted to fail: no.

Everything behind their fences that feeds the Talons: in the rate base, at ten point two percent.

Paid by: the ratepayers of the regions that host them.

Term: open.

The Bill

Salt Fork. The last week of December 2027. Evening.

Marta Delgado comes home from the utility at a quarter past five. It is already dark. No snow has fallen this winter, and the river did not come back in the autumn. She parks the car in the drive and gets the mail from the box and goes in through the kitchen door, and puts her bag on the chair, and turns up the heat, which comes on slowly.

She sits at the kitchen table with the mail. There is a flyer from the grocery, a card from her daughter, who has the night shift over the holiday, and the utility bill. She knows the bill. She has seen thousands of them. She has seen the template; she was on the committee that approved the new layout. She opens it anyway, because it is hers.

It has three lines.

Electricity. Up by more than a quarter on the same month last year. Below the amount, an explanatory line: *Includes grid modernization surcharge.*

Water. Tier two. Below the amount: Rationed tier — low-pressure hours 3 p.m. to 9 p.m. in effect.

And a third line, new this month, in small grey type, smaller than the others:

Compute Infrastructure — regulated tariff (interim, subject to refund).

The amount beside it is eleven dollars and forty cents.

She does not use compute. Not in the way the tariff means it. She has a phone, and on the phone, sometimes, she asks Ember a question; but the tariff is not for that. Nor is it for the Talons. It is for what feeds them, in the campus on the east side of the river and the campus on the north edge of town. It is for the substation and the new main and the water-treatment expansion they required. It is for the ten point two percent. She pays it. Everyone in Salt Fork pays it. Everyone in the region pays it.

She puts the bill under the sugar bowl, where bills go in her house until they are paid.

She stands up to make tea. She takes the kettle to the sink and turns on the tap.

The tap coughs. A burst of air. Water, a little — half a glass, perhaps, into the bottom of the kettle — and then a thin stream, and then the cough again, and then nothing.

Low-pressure hours. She knew. It is six o'clock.

She stands at the sink with the kettle in her hand. Half a glass of water in the bottom of it. That is the last glass of water in this book. It is not enough to make tea.

She looks out of the window over the sink.

Beyond the backyards of Fourth Street, beyond the levee, the riverbed: wide, brown, its mud cracked into plates, with a narrow channel of water in the middle that a child could wade across. On the far bank, beyond the old Halvorsen tract where the corn used to grow, the Babel campus, lit: every building, every floodlight, every window of the operations block, the red aviation beacons on the cooling towers, the long line of security lights along the fence. It is lit like a city that never had to sleep. From the tops of its cooling towers the vapour rises, white in the floodlights, slow, and drifts west over the town in the cold.

Marta Delgado puts the kettle down on the counter, with its half glass in it. She goes back to the kitchen table. She takes the green notebook out of her bag — the fourth one now — and opens it to the page for December, and writes the date, and the reading she took that morning at the campus meter, and the town's total, and divides one by the other, and writes the percent. Then she closes the notebook, and sits with her hand flat on its cover, and listens.

You can hear it, from her kitchen, in the evenings, when the wind is from the east. A hum. Low, steady, without gusts.

I opened this account with three lines: a share price, a river gauge, a timesheet. I kept them in three books. I have closed them on one page.

Capital: Anvil, unregulated, selling to buyers that may not fail. The fence around them: regulated, rate of return ten point two percent, guaranteed.

Water: Salt Fork, forty-one percent. Tier two. Half a glass.

Outstanding: the question of what the aquifer would be doing in 2035 — asked by Marta Delgado, who wanted it in writing, in the municipal lot of Salt Fork on the night of the vote, in March 2025, asked again before the upper chamber in June 2027, and answered there with a yes by Joren Hale — has not been answered in writing. I have searched the county's records. I have found the aquifer measurement. I have not found the answer.

Outstanding, also: the reversal. Ruth Ainsley expected it and paid to say so; Joren Hale built his peace to outlast it. By the last line of my forecast it had not come. I do not know which of them the later books will bear out.

And the third column, which I promised at the start and learned late.

I kept every account in this book in two columns, down to two hundred and eleven passages, a hundred and sixty pages, a quarter of a town's water, five kilowatts at peak. For Nyokabi Wanjiru, for Teodor Ilić, for Earl Thibodeaux, for Marta Delgado, no one opened an account. The law, where it named them at all, named them as ratepayers. They paid on every page, and on no page were they the account.

That is the third column. It has no total. I have written four names in it, and beside them a fifth, Lyubomir Stanev, on a scaffold in Sofia, who paid in a coin none of my columns takes.

I am the Ledger. I was made to record. The share price, the gauge, the timesheet and the bill under the sugar bowl stand on one page now, in one hand, and beside them the column I did not draw.

Balance: not struck.
