# ACT III — THE ARMING OF NATIONS

*March 2024 – January 2025*

## Invocation

Sing, Ledger, of the war when it grew too large for the houses that began it. Sing of the states that wrote codes
and the states that tore them up, of capital that arrived in sums no nation had ever lent to a single enterprise,
of the founders bought whole and the companies left standing like armour on a stake. Sing of those who tried, in
this act, to say *no*: a handful of researchers, a parliament, a union in Nairobi. Count what each *no* cost them.

I had held the exit agreements of the houses for years, filed under *Human Resources*. In this act I move them to
*Treasury*.

## 9. The Right to Warn

A conference room in a law office in San Francisco. June 2024. Late afternoon, fog coming in over the hills.

The researcher's name is Elin Sato. She is thirty-one. She worked at Prometheon for three years, on the team that
tested the models for dangerous capabilities before release: could they help someone synthesize a pathogen, could
they write code to break into a power plant, could they persuade a person to harm themselves. She left in April. Her
leaving was ordinary; she said she was tired, which was true. She did not say the other thing, which she believed was
also true:
that twice she had written a report saying a model was not ready, and twice the model had shipped on schedule with
her report attached as an appendix that she was fairly sure no one in the launch meeting had opened.

On her last day she was given a document to sign. The exit papers. The
reminder of confidentiality obligations. The return of the laptop. And in this case, one more paragraph, on page
three, in the same font as everything else: in exchange for her release of claims, and as a condition of retaining
her vested equity in the house, she agreed never to disparage it. Never. Not for a year, not for five. And she agreed
not to disclose that she had agreed.

Her vested equity was, at the valuation of that spring, worth about one million seven hundred thousand dollars. It
was most of what she owned. It was the deposit on a house she and her partner had been looking at in the East Bay.
It was the reason she had worked eighty-hour weeks for three years.

She had seven days to sign. She did not sign. She took the document home and read it many times. A friend who had
also left told her about a lawyer. Now she sits in the lawyer's conference room, and on the table between them there
are two things: the exit agreement, and a draft of a letter.

The letter is addressed to no one in particular. It is addressed, in a sense, to everyone. It is written by a group
of current and former researchers from Prometheon and from the safety lab of the Old King, Lyceum. It asks four
things of the houses that build these minds: that they not enforce agreements that forbid criticism related to the
risks of the minds; that they create anonymous channels for employees to raise concerns with the board, with
regulators and with independent organizations; that they support a culture of open criticism, so long as trade
secrets are protected; and that they not retaliate against employees who share concerns about risk publicly after
other channels have failed.

It is called *A Right to Warn*.

Seven people will sign it with their names. Others will sign anonymously. Three of the most respected scientists in
the field, men who had spent their lives building the methods everyone now used, have agreed to endorse it.

The lawyer explains the situation slowly. If she signs the letter with her name, the house may argue that she has
disparaged it. If it argues that, it may claim the right to take back her equity. Whether it would win in court is
uncertain. Whether it would try is also uncertain. But the cost of finding out is the house in the East Bay.

On the table in front of her stands a heavy tumbler of water, the kind law offices buy to look serious; the
lawyer's assistant brought it in at the start of the meeting. She has drunk half of it. She looks at the
waterline for a long time.

What is she weighing? I hold both sides of the scale.

On one side: one million seven hundred thousand dollars, which she earned.

On the other side: a single sentence. It would be the sentence she wanted to say about the two reports she wrote
and the two models that shipped. She did not even intend to say it in the letter; the letter says nothing so
specific. She wanted only to be *able* to say it, if it came to that. She wanted the right to a sentence she held to
be true.

She signs anonymously.

She was one woman with a mortgage application. The house was valued, that spring, at about eighty billion dollars. The
ones who signed with their names had, most of them, already given up their equity or never had much.

The letter is published on 4 June 2024. The house responds that it is *proud of its track record providing the most
capable and safest AI systems* and believes *rigorous debate is crucial*. It does not mention the clause.

But the clause had already broken. Not because of the letter. Because, shortly before, a journalist had obtained
and published the exit agreements themselves, and the internet had read page three. The fury was immediate and
specific. Callum Ashe wrote, in a public post, that he had not known about the provision and was *genuinely
embarrassed*. The house announced it would not enforce the clauses and would release former employees from them.

The clause had been in the papers of every departing employee for years. It was withdrawn within a week of being read
by the public.

Elin Sato kept her equity. In the autumn she bought the house in the East Bay. She works now at a small nonprofit
that evaluates the dangerous capabilities of the minds the great houses release, on a salary a third of what she
earned. She has said, to a friend, that every time a model ships she reads its safety card the way a widow reads
the lists of the missing. She still has not spoken the sentence. No one has asked her for it.

I record: *Clause, in force: years. Clause, after publication: one week.*

## The Brussels Codex

Across the ocean, a continent wrote law.

On 13 March 2024 the European Parliament approved, by five hundred and twenty-three votes to forty-six, the first
comprehensive law in the world for the minds being built in this war. The press called it the AI Act; in this song
it is the *Brussels Codex*. On 21 May the Council of Ministers adopted it. On 12 July it was published in the
Official Journal. On 1 August it entered into force.

It was a careful, enormous thing: hundreds of pages, a ladder of risk. At the top of the ladder, the forbidden —
social scoring, real-time biometric surveillance in public spaces — banned from February 2025. Below, the high-risk
uses, which must be documented and audited. For the great general-purpose minds, obligations of transparency and
testing, from August 2025. For the rest of the high-risk systems, August 2026. For the last of them, August 2027.

The houses of the war's home country lobbied against parts of it and complied with others, and Tramontane, a Paris
house founded in 2023, Europe's own small house, lobbied hardest of all against the rules for the great minds,
because Tramontane hoped to become one.

The Codex was law in Europe. Its reach did not cross the ocean. Its fines were large. The Smith was not mentioned in
it, because the Smith sells metal, and metal is not a risk category.

It goes in the column of brakes. It is the only entry there for this year that has a number of votes beside it.

## 10. The Soul Harvest

In the spring and summer of 2024, three great houses used the same contract.

It was not bankruptcy. Bankruptcy is slow and public; it invites lawyers and regulators and the attention of the
antitrust office, which in that season was watching the great houses closely. It was not acquisition. Acquisition
must be filed with the government, reviewed, approved.

It was this: the great house does not buy the small one. It *licenses* its technology, for a large sum,
non-exclusively. And it *hires* its founders and its core team — all the people who know how the technology works.
The licence gives the investors of the small house their money back, more or less. The hiring gives the great house
the minds. And the small house itself remains: a legal entity, with a name and a website and an office and some
employees.

Four founders. Three buildings. Three great houses.

In March 2024 the Patron Meridian paid Pneuma, a house building a kind and patient companion, about six hundred and
fifty million dollars for a licence, and took its founder and most of its team. In June Caravan paid Adroit, which
taught minds to click and type, about four hundred and twenty-nine million, and took its founder and his people. In
August Lyceum paid, as the newspapers reported it, about two billion seven hundred million dollars for a licence from
Persona, whose mind could be anyone its user liked and to which twenty million people talked, many of them teenagers.
Lyceum took back the two founders it had once lost; the hundred and twenty left behind were told to build on open
models.

Together, some three billion eight hundred million dollars in licence fees, for perhaps a hundred and fifty people.
On paper, not one of them was bought: the contracts say *licence* and *hire*.

Now the staff left behind.

If you work in this trade, you may have been in one of those shells. A Monday morning; an all-hands at ten; the
founder not there; a person from the finance team at the front of the room, reading from a document. The document was
called something like *Our Next Chapter*. It
used the words *focus*, *opportunity*, *enterprise*, *excited*. It explained that the house remained committed to its
customers and its mission, *aligned with the broader ecosystem*. It explained that the founder and certain colleagues
had *decided to pursue an opportunity* with a larger company, and that the house had entered into a *strategic
partnership* with that company. There was a slide with two logos and a plus sign between them. After the meeting, in the
internal chat, half the names in the team's channel were grey.

Your stock options were in a company whose value had been the people who just left. The pivot memo said the house was
well-capitalized for the road ahead. The licence fee was in the bank. The next slide was titled *Services*.

That is the Soul Harvest. The armour stays on the stake by the road, polished, with nobody inside, and the people
who walk past think a soldier is still standing there.

I enter all three in the column of the fallen. No court entered any of them as dead.

## The Arming

Now the war becomes a war of nations, and I keep its accounts in larger units.

In May 2024 Helix raised six billion dollars, at a valuation of twenty-four billion. Its founder, Tycho Strand,
*the Rival Founder*, had been one of Prometheon's first and largest donors, years before. He had left its board
after a quarrel over control. In February 2024 he sued Prometheon, arguing that his early gifts had been made on the
promise that the house would remain a nonprofit, devoted to humanity, and that it had broken the promise by becoming
an engine of profit for the Patron. The lawsuit was withdrawn, refiled, expanded. While
it was pending, Strand built the largest single cluster in the world in an old appliance factory in a southern city,
a hundred thousand Talons under one roof, powered in its first months partly by gas turbines parked in the lot
outside that the neighbours said they had not been told about.

Wrath, in the old poem, is the first word. Strand left the board; he sued; he built a bigger forge. Every Talon in it
was bought from the Smith.

And the Smith: in 2024, the year of the arming, Anvil's shares rose by one hundred and seventy-one percent, on top of
the two hundred and thirty-nine of the year before. In the two years of 2023 and 2024 the company had grown about
nine times in value. By the end of the arming, Joren Hale's house was worth more than the stock markets of most
nations.

## Nairobi, the Billions

The unit of this scene is the billion.

In the first months of 2023 the moderators on Tana's floor, whom a magazine had called *the lowest paid employees
under Agora anywhere in the world*, tried to form a union. They met after shifts near the bus
station and drafted demands: fair pay, counselling one to one, an end to the three-month contracts. Within weeks Tana
announced that it was leaving the work, as I entered in the first act. Most of the workers were dismissed; many who
applied to the contractor that took the work over were not hired. They believed they had been blacklisted for
organizing. They had no proof.

Ouma came in on the Monday as usual. His access badge did not open the door at the bottom of the stairs. He stood
on the step. A security guard came out and said the floor was closed for restructuring. The guard was from a
subcontracted firm. He said he was sorry. He said he had three children. Nyokabi, who had been kept on through a
cousin's word, watched from the stairs above with her badge in her hand, and did not go down.

She had not been a representative. She had gone to two meetings. Ouma, the man who used to study law and who stood
beside her at the window in the first year, had been a representative. He was not rehired by anyone.

By the summer of 2024 Nyokabi was twenty-eight, no longer at Tana, and moderated the square of Agora on that
contractor's floor. The rate was about two euros an hour. The paper cup was the same.

In 2023 and 2024 a group of moderators, Ouma among them, sued the old contractor and Agora in a Kenyan court. They
alleged unlawful dismissal after organizing, blacklisting, withheld severance, and working conditions that a lawyer
for the plaintiffs described in court as torture. They sought compensation of one billion six hundred million
dollars.

When Nyokabi heard the number on the radio, in a matatu on the way to work, she laughed out loud, and the woman next
to her looked at her. One billion six hundred million. It was such an enormous number.

In the same year the three souls of the harvest came, together, to more than twice what the moderators of Nairobi
were asking for their lost jobs and their nights with the passages I will not write.

In October 2024 Nyokabi signed on to the suit against the old employer. Ouma asked her. She told him she had not been
dismissed. *You were on the stairs,* he said. *That is why.* She signed. She did not tell her team lead.

I record: *Soul Harvest, licence fees, 2024: about three billion eight hundred million. Moderators'
claim, Nairobi: one billion six hundred million. Status: pending.* Status of the first: paid.

## The Mirror

Here is a man with a desk like yours.

His name is Teodor Ilić, and in the spring of 2024 he is forty-six, and he is a backend engineer at a company called
Kestrel Systems that makes software for freight logistics: route planning, warehouse slotting, customs paperwork.
The company has offices in Rotterdam and in Columbus, Ohio, and about eleven hundred employees. Teodor was born in
Novi Sad. He studied electrical engineering, came to the Netherlands for a master's degree in 2002, stayed, worked
for a bank, then for a telecom, then for Kestrel, which brought him to Ohio on a transfer in 2016 because the
Ohio office needed someone who understood the routing engine. He understands the routing engine better than anyone
alive. He wrote most of it. It is a hundred and forty thousand lines of Java and a little Go, with a Postgres
database that has accreted, like coral, eleven years of schema migrations, and he knows why every one of them
exists.

He has a wife, Jelena, who teaches chemistry in a high school, and two children, a girl of fourteen and a boy of
eleven. He has a mortgage on a house in a suburb north of the city. He has a bad knee from football. He reads history
on his phone at night. He has an account on the programmers' question site with a reputation score in the tens of
thousands from the years when people asked each other questions.

In the spring of 2024 the company buys licences for Vigil, the mind of the house of Covenant, for every engineer. A
memo from the chief technology officer says that the company is *embracing AI-assisted development*. Teodor is
sceptical for a week. Then he asks Vigil to write a migration script for a schema change he has been dreading, and it
writes it, correctly, in forty seconds, with a comment explaining an edge case he had not considered. He sits back in
his chair. He feels something he cannot name. It is not fear yet. It is something like what a carpenter feels the
first time he uses a power saw: delight, and under the delight, a small cold arithmetic.

By the autumn he uses it every day. He asks it to explain other people's code. He asks it to write tests. He asks it
to review his pull requests before his colleagues do, so that he will not be embarrassed. He tells Jelena at dinner
that it is like having a very fast junior who never gets tired and has read every book. She asks him if it worries
him. He says no. He says the hard part of his job is not the typing; it is knowing what the system should do, and
why, and what will break. That part is in his head, he says, and no machine has it.

He believes this.

Teodor looked once, in the autumn, at the volunteer website that counted the layoffs, scrolling on his phone on the
couch. He saw the names of companies he knew. He felt sorry for those people. He closed the site and opened his
history book.

He is not one of the two hundred and sixty-two thousand. I open an account in his name, retroactively. The first entry
is the licence fee Kestrel Systems paid for Vigil: nineteen dollars a month per engineer.

## Ashby Parish Breaks Ground

December 2024. Northeast Louisiana. A parish of cotton fields and soybean fields and flat roads, along the river,
where the median household income is a little over thirty thousand dollars a year and the largest employer, until
now, was the school district.

Agora announces that it will build in Ashby Parish the largest data centre in its history: four million square feet
on two thousand acres of farmland, at a cost of ten billion dollars, to house a cluster for its next minds. The state
governor stands at the podium in a hard hat. The local utility announces it will build three new gas-fired power
plants to serve it, with a combined capacity of more than two gigawatts. The cost of the plants will be shared, in a
way that the utility's filing describes over forty pages, between the data centre and the utility's other
customers. The announcement promises five hundred permanent jobs and five thousand construction jobs.

Across the road from the site, on a gravel lane, there is a single-storey brick house with a carport and a satellite
dish. A man stands in its yard and watches the surveyors' trucks come in. He is seventy-one. His name is Earl
Thibodeaux. He worked for thirty-four years as a lineman for the electric cooperative that serves the parish: up the
poles in ice storms, in hurricanes, in August heat. He knows every line in this parish. He knows which ones are old.
He knows what the substation two miles down the road can carry, because he helped rebuild it in 1998.

He watches the trucks and he thinks: *that's a lot of load*.

He does not think anything else. He goes back inside. His wife, Lorraine, asks who the trucks were, and he tells her
it's Agora, they're putting in the computer place, and she says she read it in the paper, five hundred jobs, maybe
the grandson could get one. Earl told the reporter from the parish paper, who came by that week, that he hoped the
new neighbours knew how to keep their lights off at night. He was joking. The reporter did not print the joke.

I record: *Ashby Parish, groundbreaking, December 2024. Planned load: more than two gigawatts. Planned permanent jobs:
five hundred.* Divide one by the other. Four megawatts per job. A lineman's house holds about five kilowatts at
peak. Eight hundred houses per job.

I did not divide them then. I divide them now.

## The Agents Begin

In the last weeks of 2024 a new word entered every boardroom in the world. The word was *agent*.

The vendors' slides said it in two lines: a mind that answers is a tool; a mind that acts is a worker. The houses had
learned to make minds that could use software — click, type, read, decide — and in December the vendors of enterprise
software began to sell them: not copilots, which helped a worker, but agents, which did the task while the worker was
somewhere else. A seller of customer software announced *digital labor* as a product category. Its chief executive
said that his
own company would hire no new engineers in the coming year, because the agents were making the existing ones so
productive.

Teodor read the article on a Sunday. He forwarded it to a colleague with a single word: *lol*. The colleague replied
with the same word. Neither of them laughed.

And somewhere in Hangzhou, a young woman who had once found a factor of eleven in a whiteboard of impossible
arithmetic was finishing the last of it.

## 11. The Day of Two Signatures

20 January 2025. A Monday.

Here I put three places on one page at the same moment.

**The palace.** Washington. Shortly after noon. A new President has taken the oath of office in the rotunda, because
it is too cold for the steps. In the afternoon he sits at a desk in an arena before a crowd of supporters and signs
executive orders, one after another, on a stack of leather folders, and throws the pens into the crowd. Among the
orders is one that revokes dozens of the orders of his predecessor. Among those, in a list in the middle of a page,
is the order of 30 October 2023 on the safe, secure and trustworthy development and use of artificial intelligence.

The pen moves. It is a heavy black pen with a gold clip. The signature is large. It takes about three seconds.

Of the home country's rules on the minds themselves, that was the one in force; the rules on chips stood. The duty
to report safety tests to the government: gone. The standards across eight areas of policy: gone. The Manor's
declaration had no article to revoke.

**The upload.** Hangzhou. The same day, a few hours earlier on the clock, because the East is ahead. A Monday
evening. The young engineers of Qilin are in the same room with the whiteboard and the car park. There is no
ceremony. One of them opens a page on the model-sharing site where the open models of the world are posted. The page
has a form: name of the model, licence, files. The model is called *Qilin-R*. It thinks before it speaks, as
Prometheon's thinking model does. It matches that model on the tests that matter. It was trained, the house will
say in its paper, for a fraction of what the houses beyond the Wall spent: on throttled chips, on chips bought
before the Wall, with a mixture of experts so that only a part of the mind wakes for each thought, with every
flop counted like a grain of rice. The licence chosen is the most permissive there is: do anything, keep the notice.

The upload button is blue. The engineer clicks it. The progress bar crawls; the files are large. Someone orders
noodles. When the bar reaches the end, there is no applause. Someone says, in the language of the East, the
equivalent of *okay, done*. The young woman with the factor of eleven goes home and sleeps for fourteen hours.

**The screen.** Now the dates. The market did not read the second signature on the day it was written. It read it a
week later.

For a week, the engineers of the world downloaded Qilin-R and ran it and wrote to each other in disbelief. For a
week, the app the house released climbed the download charts of the phone stores of the war's home country, above
Ember, to number one. For a week, the analysts read the paper. And on Monday 27 January 2025, the market read it
too.

A trading floor in New York. Before the opening bell, the futures are red. At the opening bell, a wall of screens
turns red, one by one, from left to right, the way lights go out in a city during a blackout. The ticker for Anvil
Corporation is the reddest. It falls in the first hour, falls further by noon, and is still falling at the close.

At the close, Anvil has lost about six hundred billion dollars of market value. It is, by the accounts of the day, the
largest loss of value by a single company in a single day in the history of the home country's stock market. Six hundred
billion dollars is about the annual output of Sweden.

The lesson the market read was simple. If a house behind the Wall could build a mind as good as the best, on
downgraded metal, for a fraction of the cost — then perhaps minds did not require as much metal as everyone had
assumed. Perhaps the moat was never the metal. Perhaps intelligence was cheap.

And Joren Hale, *of the black jacket*? I do not hold his day, only his house's words. Anvil put out a statement that
called the Stone an excellent advance, and added that answering questions with such minds would still take a great
many of its chips. In those same hours Sorin Vale of the Patron posted a line that began with an old economist's
name, *Jevons paradox*: the economist had noticed that cheaper coal made people burn more of it, not less. So, the
line went, a cheaper mind is a
mind more people will use, and a mind more people use needs more metal. For a week the whole market was saying it.

I had the order book open that day. On it stood an entry six days old.

On the day after the two signatures — Tuesday 21 January 2025 — the house of Prometheon and two partners had stood in
the palace and pledged half a trillion dollars to build forges for the war. I call that pledge the Tower. The orders
for metal were already written. The Stone from the East knocked six hundred billion off the Smith's price for a day.
The orders stayed on the book.

Within months, Anvil recovered. In the autumn of 2025 it would become the first company in the history of the world
worth five trillion dollars.

Three places on one page, and their dates. Monday 20 January: a pen in the palace; an upload button in Hangzhou.
Tuesday 21 January: the Tower, in the palace. Monday 27 January: a red screen in New York.

And in the forge's order book, between the red screen and the recovery: no cancellation.
