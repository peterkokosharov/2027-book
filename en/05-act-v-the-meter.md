## Invocation, and a Seam

I must tell you plainly where I am. Everything I have recorded until now has been entered after it happened: a
share price at the close, a vote at 10:42 p.m., a calendar invitation at 9:14. I have the receipts. From this line
onward, I do not. The record I hold ends in the autumn of 2026. What follows is what my columns say *will* be
entered, if the sums go on as they have gone — a forecast, written in the same flat hand as the history, so that you
can test whether the hand has changed. A ledger that projects is still a ledger. But it is a ledger that may be
wrong, and you should read the rest of this song the way an auditor reads a budget: as the most honest guess of a
clerk who has seen every previous year's books.

Before I cross: in the autumn of 2026 the other books were full of the markets of nations that do not appear in this
song. Their clerks keep them. I carry across only what someone in this book has paid for.

Sing, then, of the winter when a state said *no more water*; of the early summer when the wires ran out; of the
hearing in the marble room where everyone said yes. Sing of how the war was ended, in a committee room, by a tariff.
Sing of the Smith's peace.

## The Moratorium

Salt Fork. Winter 2026–2027.

The Babel campus on the old Halvorsen tract was energized in its first phase in the autumn of 2026. The closed-loop
cooling system that was to *dramatically reduce water use after the first phase* is scheduled for the third phase.
The third phase is scheduled for 2029.

In the first winter, Marta Delgado's green notebook — now three notebooks, rubber-banded together — records the new
campus's draw beside the old one. Lyceum's campus, on the north edge: still about a quarter of the town's treated
water. The Babel campus, on the east side of the river: in its first full month of operation, about a sixth. Together:
about forty-one percent. The meatpacker, which has automated, uses less than it used to. The high school pool has
been closed for budget reasons. The town itself, its fifty-eight thousand people, its kitchens and showers and
gardens, gets the rest.

In September 2026, the commissioners voted to move the town to tier two of its water rate schedule: residents who
used more than a fixed monthly allowance would pay a higher rate per gallon, and the town's water pressure would be
reduced during peak hours. The commissioners described it as *shared sacrifice*. The campuses paid a fixed rate under
their water-service agreements and were not subject to tier two.

In the same September her son, who drives a grain truck out of Salt Fork, put his fuel receipt on her kitchen table
at Sunday dinner. The strait had closed again in July, and the national average for diesel that week was about six
and a half dollars a gallon,
three quarters more than a year before. He is paid by the load, not by the gallon. *The first two hours are the
truck's,* he told her. *After that I start.* Marta copied the receipt into the back of the green notebook, on the page
where she keeps the numbers that are not water.

The river, that winter, does not recover from the drought of the summer. The aquifer that the town's backup wells
draw from has fallen eleven feet in four years. The state's department of natural resources, reviewing the permits
for four further campuses proposed in the river basin, does something no state in the home country had yet done:
in February 2027, the governor, on the department's advice, signs an emergency order declaring a moratorium on new
large industrial water withdrawals for data centres in the basin, pending a study.

I enter it in the column of brakes. It is the first entry there that stops a forge.

The moratorium does not apply to campuses already built or already permitted. Lyceum's campus is grandfathered. The
Babel campus is grandfathered.

Babel had planned four more campuses in the basin. Under the order it can build none of them. In the same winter, in
three other states, legislators introduce bills modelled on the Salt Fork order.

The houses of the war do not fight it in the way they fought the Brussels Codex. They do something else. They go to
the capital and they say: *the problem is not us. The problem is that there is no national plan.* The problem is that
fifty states have fifty rules, and the grid is regional and the rivers cross borders, and the war — the national
competition, the race with the East — cannot be won if every river town can stop it. What is needed, they say, is a
national framework. A federal standard. A way of treating compute as what it has become: not a product, not a
service, but infrastructure. Like the railroads. Like the telephone. Like electricity itself.

And there, in the corridors of the capital, in the spring of 2027, the drafting of a national framework begins.

## The Labeller's Account

Nairobi. Spring 2027.

Nyokabi Wanjiru is thirty. She has not worked for a contractor since the middle of 2026.

The work did not end with a letter. It thinned. In 2025 her queue was full; in early 2026 it was half full; by the
spring of 2026 there were days when it loaded empty and the team lead told them to wait, and they waited at their
desks for hours, unpaid, because they were paid by the passage now and not by the hour. In June the contractor told
them that the client had *transitioned to automated annotation pipelines*, and that their contracts would not
be renewed.

She had watched it go from others first. In March 2024 a platform that paid Kenyans by the task, five hundred to a
thousand shillings a day, shut out every worker in the country overnight, for *enhanced security protocols*. Two
months before her own notice, across the city, one thousand one hundred and eight people lost their places on one
floor when a client ended its contract.

She knew what *automated annotation pipelines* meant before Ouma explained it to her. The models now labelled their
own training data. A mind, trained to recognize harm by the labels that Nyokabi and thousands like her had applied,
night after night, at under two euros an hour, now read the new passages itself and applied the labels itself. It did
it faster than she did. It did it for less. It did it without ever seeing the girl and the river when it closed its
eyes, because it had no eyes and did not close them.

Every one of her two hundred-odd passages a night, every category she chose, every severity she marked, had
gone into the training data of the mind that now did the work. She had been paid for each one, at the rate on the
timesheet.

The suit against the old contractor and Agora is still before the courts. The claim is still one billion six hundred
million dollars. Its hearings are adjourned and resumed and adjourned. Ouma has become, through the suit, a kind of
lawyer after all; he never finished his degree, but he knows the procedure now better than some of the advocates.
He tells Nyokabi that they will win something, someday. She believes he believes it.

She has a job, of a kind: she sells airtime and phone accessories from a kiosk near the bus station in Embakasi, the
station where she once caught the matatu home at dawn. The chargers come from the East in cartons, each carton cheaper
than the last. Her margin on a charger thins with the price. She earns about what she earned on the night shift, on
a good week. She sleeps at night now. Most nights. The boyfriend went in the second year of the night shift; she
does not blame him, and does not speak of it.

Her cousin's daughter finishes her diploma this year and comes to the kiosk to ask how to get on a floor like
Nyokabi's. Nyokabi tells her what it paid, and what it bought in Murang'a, and that the floors she knew are closed.
The girl asks where the work went. Nyokabi points at the phone in the girl's hand.

In April 2026, while the strait was still all but closed and she still had a queue, the price of fuel in Kenya rose
to a record, and
the fares of the matatus rose by about a quarter. The ride to the station that had cost her twenty-odd minutes of
the night shift's pay now cost her nearly half an hour. On the evenings when Mombasa Road locked solid at the hour
the city went home, she got down at the flyover with the fare already paid and walked the last of it, and was home
before the matatu.

In September the world's index of food prices stood at its
highest since the first autumn of her night shift, and its cooking oils nearly a fifth above the year before. She
still sends her mother in Murang'a the money for the pills, one box. When she visits, she brings oil and flour from
Nairobi. She brings less of it. Her mother weighs the bag in her hand and says: *Bring less, then. Come more.* Now
that her days are her own,
she does.

One evening, in the spring, a young man buys a phone charger from her and, while she counts his change, asks her
something about it — whether it will work with his model — and she does not know, so she asks Ember on her own
phone. Ember answers politely. It is helpful. There is nothing in its answer that should not be there.

She looks at the gentle answer on her screen for a moment longer than she needs to.

The next week she goes to the union office above the pharmacy on Mombasa Road, where Ouma is gathering statements for
the suit. The woman who keeps the membership book fills in the form for her. Years of work: four, nights for the first
two. Member: yes. Plaintiff: yes. Then the line for her trade. The woman reads out the list printed on the form,
*clerical, data entry, call centre, other*, and waits with the pen.

*Write teacher,* Nyokabi says. *It learned from me.*

The woman looks at Ouma. Ouma nods. She writes it.

I record: *Nyokabi Wanjiru. Hours labelled, 2022–2026: about six thousand. Rate: a little under two euros. Status:
replaced by the model trained on her labels.*

## The Contractor

Columbus. Spring 2027.

Teodor Ilić is forty-nine. He has a contract.

It came, in the end, from Kestrel Systems. Seven months after his layoff, the routing engine failed in a way the
documentation did not cover — a fourth kind of database corruption, triggered by a schema migration the agent had
written itself, correctly, in a way that interacted with the Mexican customer's `legacy_flag_2` in a pattern that no
one had foreseen because no one had ever had to. The agent tried to fix it for nineteen hours, by the logs. It made it
worse. On the second day, Teodor's former manager's manager called him personally and asked if he would be willing to
help, *as a consultant*.

He fixed it in four hours. He billed for eight.

Now he works for Kestrel two days a week, from the kitchen table, his bad knee up on the second chair, as an
independent contractor, through an agency that takes a cut. His title
on the agency's paperwork is *AI Operations Supervisor — Logistics Systems*. His job is to watch the agents that do the
work he used to do: to review their changes, to catch what they miss, to be the person who knows why. He is paid
fifty-eight dollars an hour, before the agency's cut, and has no benefits, and no equity, and no pension contributions,
and no guarantee of hours. Before, he cost the company eighty-two dollars an hour, plus all those things. The agents
do ninety percent of what he used to do. He does the ten percent that is left. The contract is for two days a week.

He tells Jelena that it is fine. *I'm the ten percent,* he says. *The part they call when it breaks.* She knows it is
not fine, and that it is not the worst, and moves his
coffee within reach of the knee. The worst is his friend
from Rotterdam, who was let go the same week, and who has not found anything, and who drives for a delivery company
whose routes are planned by software that Teodor wrote.

On the first Sunday of each month Jelena puts the household on the kitchen table, in a spreadsheet on her laptop,
the way she enters grades. There are seven lines. The mortgage, fixed in 2016: the only line that has not moved. Her
salary, which rose three percent last year, while the things on the other lines rose faster. His contract, which
varies. The card, which they paid off every month for eleven years and which, since the summer of his layoff, they
have not. The home-equity line they opened to cover the months between his severance and his first invoice, at a
little over
eight percent, floating with the central bank's rate. Fuel, for
two cars and her drive to Galena Crossing. And the electricity, which has two new lines of its own.

Their daughter is looking at rooms near the university for the autumn. The agent she asks tells her that rents are
flat this year. By the last count, almost half of the renting households in the home country paid more than
three tenths of their income in rent, more than ever recorded.

Every few months the agency asks him to update the documentation. He does. He is careful. He is good at his work.
*Write it worse,* Jelena says once, at the table, over the seven lines. He laughs. Then he sees that she meant it. He
tries, one evening, with a section on the Belgian port, and deletes the draft before midnight. He does not know how.

Once, in the spring, the agent proposes a change to the customs module, the one with the special case for the port in
Belgium. He reviews it. He finds nothing to change. He writes *approved* in the review field. Then he sits for a while
looking at the word.

The agents had quoted *Things that will look wrong and are not* in their first week of operation. I have the log.

I record: *Teodor Ilić. Rate, 2025: eighty-two dollars an hour, salaried. Rate, 2027: fifty-eight dollars an hour,
contract, two days a week. Supervising: the agents trained on his documentation.*

## The Scaffold

Sofia. Summer 2026 to spring 2027.

Lyubomir Stanev's son married in July 2026; the gold for the rings cost about two and a half times what it had cost
in the year the fire was lit. The young couple rent at the edge of Sofia.

In March 2027 a message comes from a man Lyubomir worked with for nine years, who now finds reviewers for an agency
with clients in Rotterdam and Ohio. The agency needs people who can read code and approve what the agents write:
remote, by the hour, at about three times what the scaffold pays. *You were always the one who read everything,* the
message says. *They need that now.*

The account is a maker of software for freight, and the field he would sign in is the one where, in Columbus, Teodor
Ilić writes *approved* two days a week. Neither man knows the other's name. I hold both.

That evening he does the sum on a torn piece of cement bag, with the flat pencil he marks the boards with: the
difference would pay his son's rent and leave something over. He tells Deyan at lunch on the boards. Deyan says take
it, are you mad. Lyubomir says he will think.

He thinks for a week, out loud, in pieces, to Deyan, over several lunches, with the tea going cold in the flask cap
between his gloves. He says the bill for all of it has come, and nobody at the top wants to pay it, so they make wars
and thin the money, and the ordinary man gains a little and pays at every till. Deyan asks what he means to do about
it, then. *Pay,* Lyubomir says. *At the till. Like everyone.*

Then who won, Deyan asks. Lyubomir counts it off on his glove: the ones who build; the young in Nairobi, for a few
good years. Here the young lose first. His old firm took four juniors every autumn; in 2026 it took none. *We were
the last up the ladder,* he says. *Then they pulled it up.* Deyan, the youngest on the boards, looks at his anchors.

On another day he knocks on a pole with his knuckle. There was a king in Babylon, he says, or a chief in Provadiya,
by the salt. If a builder builds a house and the house falls and kills its owner, the builder dies. *My trade,* he
says. *Hard, but you could read it.* You govern, but you answer. *Now you govern, and someone else answers.* A ruler
who did not answer paid with his head, or his throne, and a firm that failed went bankrupt; rescues were exceptions.
The break was 2008: the losses were made common, the bonuses stayed private. 2020 finished it, with money that did
not run out. Now the rescue is planned before the fall. *Iceland put its bankers in prison,* he says, *and Iceland is
still there.* *This time is different* and *too big to fail* are the same sentence, he says, said by different
people, on the way up and on the way down.

Deyan asks what that has to do with programs. A shop he built for a client in Hamburg, Lyubomir says, ran in a box
inside a box inside a third. Docker in docker in docker. No one could say what the bottom box did. *And the bill?*
Deyan asks. *Whoever comes tomorrow,* Lyubomir says.

On the last day, with the flask in his hand, he says: *Will there be anything human in it? That's all I want to
know.*

Deyan says that is a lot of philosophy for a scaffold.

I hold the lines he leaned on, not the line he drew through them. Against *gains a little* I hold a Sunday in
Murang'a, of which he has never heard. For *make wars* I have no column.

On the Friday morning the boards are white with frost. Lyubomir checks the first anchor of the day, clips on, and leans
back into the harness over eight floors of air, as he does every morning, and the anchor holds, as it does. Then he
takes off one glove and writes to his old colleague with his thumb. He thanks him. He says no. He gives no reason in
the message, because the reason is small. When he sets an anchor, he can put his weight on it and know. In the review
field he would write *approved* over work he could not put his weight on, and be paid well for the word.

He puts the glove back on before he tells them. Deyan, for once, says nothing. Kiril, who came home by letter and
stays by choice, sets down his float. *At least it is your no,* he says. *Mine came in the post.*

He is not sure he is right. He says so to Deyan. He says he may be a fool, and a poorer one. He does not tell his son.

I enter what it cost him: about two thirds of what he could have earned, each month, from that spring. On the other
side I have nothing to enter. I have no column for what he kept.

## The Heat

Late May and June 2027.

A dome of high pressure settles over the middle of the continent and does not move for nineteen days. In Caldera,
Texas, the temperature reaches a hundred and fourteen degrees Fahrenheit on the sixth day. In Salt Fork it passes a
hundred and five on eleven days.

The forges do not slow down. They cannot. Every hour of their compute has been sold, many times over, to houses that
have sold it again to their own customers. And in the heat they need more power, not less, because their cooling must
work harder.

On the eleventh day, at 4:52 in the afternoon, the operator of the grid that serves the plains declares an energy
emergency and orders rolling outages: blocks of customers cut off in rotation, an hour at a time, to keep the whole
system from collapsing. The order covers homes and shops. It does not cover the campuses, which are under contract as
*non-interruptible critical loads*, and which in any case have their own generation behind the fence.

Fourth Street is in block seven. In the late afternoons the fan in Marta Delgado's kitchen stops, and the refrigerator,
and the street goes quiet, and people sit on their porches in the heat. The widow two doors down is eighty-four
and will not leave her house. At five each day Marta takes her a wet towel and a jug of water and sits with her through
the hour. Across the river the campus hums.

Marta's daughter, the nurse, works the outage days at her hospital in the capital. Its corridors fill with the old.
The coroners will
attribute twenty-three deaths in the region to the heat. Whether the outages killed any of them is not established.

In the capital of the nation, the hearings on the national framework, which had been planned for the autumn, are
brought forward to June.

## The Smith's Night

Washington. The night before the hearing. June 2027.

Here I break my own rule further than anywhere else in this book, and say so. I have no record of a man's night. I
hold what Joren Hale has said in
public over thirty years, what his house has done, and what he will say in the morning. From these I reconstruct what
he must weigh, the way an auditor rebuilds a missing ledger from the ones that stand beside it. Read what follows as my
reconstruction, not as his mind. I write it because without it the peace that follows would be only my verdict, and you
are owed his case, in the strongest form I can give it, before you weigh the rest.

A hotel suite a few streets from the marble room. The aides have gone. On the desk: the draft bill, a hundred and
twelve pages; the list of the morning's witnesses, on which he has read the words *lineman, retired* and *utility
clerk*; a quarterly letter from a fund manager in Boston, forwarded by his own staff with the note *for awareness*;
and a
single sheet of paper with one line printed on it.

He has stood at this edge before. In the autumn of 2022 his shares were at a hundred and eight dollars and the analysts
wrote *glut*. He knows better than the woman in Boston that the seller of picks is the first to starve when the rush
ends. He has read her letter to the end. He thinks she is right about the wear on
the metal, half right about the circle, and wrong about the only thing that matters, which is what comes after.

Of the man outside the house I hold little, and all of it is his own telling, in public. He has said that if he had
known
what building his house would cost, he would not have started it. I enter it as his words, not as his heart.

His case, as I can rebuild it from his own words, runs like this.

Every age has overbuilt its infrastructure, and every time some of the overbuilding was madness. The canal companies
failed and the water stayed in the canals. The railway shares of the 1840s ruined a generation
of investors, and the rails carried a continent's grain for a century. The fibre laid in the dotcom years was sold for
cents after the crash and carried the world's voices for twenty years after. That is how a civilization pays for what it
needs and cannot price in advance: by letting its investors be wrong in the direction of building. The bank of the
central banks set this build-out beside those. He does not dispute the comparison. He accepts it. He thinks it is the
argument for him.

Second. If the reversal comes, and he does not say it will not, who is left holding the fences? Without a law, a campus
whose tenant fails is an empty building, a stranded bond, and a town that had budgeted for its taxes: Salt Fork with a
dark fence and a water main built for a customer who has gone. Under a utility the campus is part of the rate base. It
is maintained. Its taxes are paid. Its water plant does not rust. The towns the woman in Boston worries about are safer
under his law than under her crash. Eleven dollars a month, he would say, is cheaper than an empty fence.

Third. The chips are not infrastructure. They change every year; that is the whole point of them. Fix the price of the
newest accelerator and you freeze it. The price of an answer from a mind has fallen many times over in three years
because his house was free to make it fall. The wires and the pipes have always been paid for by everyone, because
everyone uses them, and what runs on them will be used by everyone too: a woman in a village checking her pills, a girl
choosing a university, a clerk who asks a question on her phone.

Fourth. Every country will need its own. A nation that does not own its compute will rent its intelligence from one that
does, the way nations without oil rented their energy for a century. If the home country will not build its own, the
East will build the world's.

And last, the part he will not say at the table. Yes, it is good for Anvil. He has never pretended otherwise. In his
experience what he has built that was good for the world was also good for his company, and he does not see why that
should count against it. A man who sells the tools for a rush, and asks the government to make sure the towns are not
left with holes in the ground, is not, in his arithmetic, a villain. He is the only one at the table who has thought
past
the rush.

He turns the sheet face down, so that he will not read it again before morning, and turns out the light.

That is his case, as fairly as I can enter it. I have looked for a false line in it and found none I can prove false.
There is one line I could not find in it, and could not find anywhere else in this book either: who pays, if he is wrong
about what comes after. I will not weigh it for you.

## The Hearing

Washington. June 2027.

A committee room of the upper chamber of the national legislature: marble, walnut, a raised dais in a horseshoe,
the senators' names on brass plates, a gallery behind a rail. Television cameras on tripods. A witness table with a
green cloth and five microphones and five glasses of water, poured from a jug by a page before the session. The
hearing is titled *Powering American Leadership: Compute as Critical National Infrastructure*. It is the third of
four. A draft bill sits on every senator's desk, a hundred and twelve pages. It has been circulated, the trade press
reports, to *stakeholders* for comment.

The chairman opens. He is a senator from a state with many forges, a man with a pleasant voice and a gift for phrases.
He speaks of national competitiveness. He speaks of the race with the East. He speaks of growth. Behind him, on a
large screen installed for the hearing, a chart: the gross domestic product of the nation, quarter by quarter, since
2022, with the contribution of investment in compute shaded in a different colour. The line goes up. He says that this
is the most important economic transformation since electrification, and that it cannot be allowed to be stopped by *a
patchwork of local objections*.

He says that he has asked to hear from Americans whose communities have been affected.

**The first witness is Earl Thibodeaux.**

He is seventy-three. He wears a brown suit that he bought for his daughter's wedding in 2009, and it is loose on him
now,
because Lorraine died in the spring of 2026, three months after the ice night, of pneumonia that followed the
emphysema, and since then he has not eaten much. Her death was not caused by the four days. Her doctor said so. Her
doctor also said the four days did not help.

In his jacket pocket, folded with the typed page, is the mail of the week before. The statement from the
cooperative's retirement plan: his account rose last year; the plan holds an index fund, and among the largest names
in the index is Anvil. The electricity bill: up, with the surcharge for the new lines. And the yearly statement of his
old-age pension, with its line that the fund which pays it is expected to run short in the last quarter of 2032,
after which it can pay a little under four fifths of what it promises. He will be close to eighty. The fund's gap can
be closed only by a vote of this legislature. The interest on the nation's debt is paid without one. He did not bring
those papers to read from. They are in his pocket because that is where he put the mail.

He reads from a single typed page that a woman from the community group in the church helped him prepare. His hands
shake a little. His voice does not.

He says that he worked for thirty-four years for the electric cooperative in Ashby Parish. He says he has climbed
poles in more ice storms than anyone on this committee has seen. He says that in January 2026 his house was without
power for four days, and that during those four days the data centre across the
road from his house, which belongs to Agora, was lit every hour of every day and every night, and he could hear it.

He says: "I'm not here to tell you that the data centre took our power. I can't prove that. I've been a lineman. I
know you can't prove that from a kitchen window. The utility says it didn't, and I don't have their papers. Maybe it
didn't."

He stops. He looks at the page. He looks up.

"But I sat in my kitchen with my wife on oxygen, and I melted ice off my porch on a camp stove to make her a cup of
tea, and I looked out the window at a building that had more power than God, and I could hear it humming. And
no crew — not one — came to my house for four days. And I know where the crews were. Everybody in the parish knows
where the crews were."

He says: "You're going to write a law. I'd ask you to write in it that when the ice comes, the people go first."

He sits down.

The chairman thanks him for his service and his testimony and says that the committee is deeply sorry for his loss.
A senator from a state without forges asks him two kind questions. A senator from a state with many forges asks the
utility's representative, later, whether the data centre had drawn from the residential distribution system during
the event. The representative says it had not. The question is closed.

**The second witness is Marta Delgado.**

She is fifty-five. She is still Senior Utility Clerk of Salt Fork. She has brought the green notebooks, all three,
rubber-banded together, and they sit on the green cloth in front of her, and she does not open them, because she does
not need to.

She has been allowed, this time, to speak the numbers. The moratorium order and its study made them public in April:
the campuses' water-service agreements were released by the state under its emergency powers. She has her own
figures and the official figures and they match, to within two percent, and she is quietly proud of that, though she
does not say so.

She says that one campus has taken about a quarter of Salt Fork's drinking water since before 2023, and that the
second, the Babel campus, approved by four votes to three on the promise of sixty permanent jobs, takes about a sixth
more: forty-one percent together. She says the aquifer under the town has fallen eleven feet in four years, and that
the river last August was the lowest in the utility's records, which go back to 1931. She says that most households
now reach the second tier of the water price by the middle of each month, and with it the low-pressure hours of the
late afternoon and evening, when the campuses' cooling demand is highest.

She says: "I've read the meters in Salt Fork for twenty-three years. I'm not against the data centres. They pay their
bills. I know, because I send them." A little laughter. "But I'd like this committee to know that when you say
*growth*" — she does not look at the chart, but everyone else does — "it's not coming out of nowhere. Some of it is
coming out of our river. And our river doesn't grow."

She says she has one more question, which she has written on an index card, in pencil. She reads it.
*What will the aquifer be doing in 2035? I asked the county in 2025 and I asked the state in 2026. I would like it
in writing.* She puts the card down on the notebooks.

She sits down. She takes a sip of her water.

**Two witnesses for the industry follow**, a former undersecretary and the chief of a regional grid operator. They
speak of *reliability*, *investment certainty* and *cost recovery mechanisms*, and say that without a national
framework the race with the East will be lost.

**The last witness is Joren Hale.**

He comes in through a side door, with two aides, and the cameras turn. He is sixty-four. He is wearing the black
jacket. He has worn it in public for so long that the room does not remark on a leather jacket at a hearing of the
national legislature.

He sits. He thanks the chairman. He has no written statement. He has a single sheet of paper, which he places face
down on the green cloth, beside his glass. Then he waits. My columns put the wait at ninety
seconds, in which the chairman shuffles his notes and the cameras hold on the jacket.

Then Joren Hale says: *Yes.*

The chairman says, pleasantly, that he has not yet asked a question.

*Yes, I know. I would like to answer the questions you were going to ask, all of them, before you ask them. Yes to
oversight. Yes to a public-interest standard. Yes to a tariff that reflects the cost of the infrastructure we are
asking the public to host. Yes to the metering of every intake. Yes to water reporting in real time. Yes to the
reviews of the permits. Yes to the question about 2035. Yes to the question the witness asked at the table before
mine. The one in writing.*

The chairman asks whether he is prepared to commit his company to all of that, on the record.

He says that he is. He says that Anvil supports federal regulation of the facilities designated as critical national
compute infrastructure — their siting, their power, their water, the terms on which they buy — and will offer those
facilities long-term supply at its published prices, and a share of its supply to public research. He says that the
age of intelligence will be built on infrastructure, like every age before it, and that infrastructure is the
responsibility of the whole nation. He says that a campus under a utility is kept up and taxed even if its tenant
fails, and that no town should be left holding an empty fence.

Then he picks up the sheet of paper and turns it over and reads the single line printed on it: *Proposed definition:
compute infrastructure as critical public utility.* He says that a draft of the technical standards that would go
with it is available to the committee's staff on request. Anvil's engineers have, in fact, already assisted the staff
with Title III of the bill, which defines what counts as a critical compute facility and what equipment may be
installed in one. He says he is not claiming authorship. He says that this is what it means to be a responsible
partner to the nation.

Yes to oversight. Yes to tariffs. Yes to the public interest. Yes to everything.

Anvil's share price that morning, as my columns project it, was trading slightly down when he sat. The rise began at the
moment Joren Hale said the word *Yes* for the fourth time. By the close it was up four percent: more than two hundred
billion dollars, in a few minutes of testimony and a few hours of trading. The analysts' notes that evening used one
word again and again. The word was *visibility*.

Callum Ashe, *the twice-crowned*, was not called to testify. His house had been eclipsed by Covenant. It had signed
take-or-pay commitments to buy compute from Babel's campuses at a fixed rate for ten years, whether it used the compute
or not; the Tower's debt was secured by those commitments; the campuses were filled with Anvil's metal; and among its
shareholders, since April 2026, was the Smith himself, with thirty billion dollars' worth of its future.

In the old poem the smith of the gods forges the chains that bind Prometheus to the rock, and an eagle comes to him
every day. In my books: the chain, a thirty-billion stake; the rock, a ten-year take-or-pay; the daily entry, *capex*.

Darius Merrow, *the careful*, sent written testimony. It was entered into the record. No one read it aloud.

I read it. I read everything. Here is the last paragraph of it:

*I support oversight of the infrastructure on which these systems run. But I want to say clearly that this bill
regulates the power stations, not what the power is used for. It makes the supply of compute stable and permanent. It
says nothing about the minds being built with it, or how fast, or whether the people building them should be permitted
to stop. We founded our company on the belief that safety must be at the frontier. I am no longer certain what the
frontier is. I now believe the frontier is the wrong place to measure it, because the frontier is measured in
megawatts, and the megawatts are measured in water. I am not certain whether any of us is still able to choose not to
cross it. I would ask the committee to consider that question, too, before it guarantees the fuel.*

The question was not on the agenda.

Elin Sato watched the hearing with her coat still on, at the nonprofit where she reads the safety cards. No one
called her.
No title of the bill had a place for the sentence she still has not spoken.

Ilan Voss, *of both letters*, was not called and did not write. Still Water had raised more money and still released
nothing. On its balance sheet there was still no water.

The bill was reported out of the committee at the end of June, by a vote of fifteen to nine. It did not pass that
summer; bills of a hundred and twelve pages seldom do, and the lower chamber had a bill of its own. In the autumn the
leaders of both chambers did what is done with a bill that cannot pass alone. In the first week of December they folded
it, as one title among many, into the yearly bill that pays for the armies, which members of either party vote against
only at a price. With the heat of June still in the speeches, it passed both chambers in that form. The President
signed it in the second week of December, in the room with the portraits, with Joren Hale behind his right shoulder and
the governor of the state that contains Salt Fork behind his left.

The law declared the compute facilities critical national infrastructure. It established a federal commission to
regulate it. It preempted the state moratoriums, replacing them with a federal process for siting and water allocation
that the river towns could petition but not veto. It created, in Title IV, a *compute infrastructure tariff*: a charge
on the bills of all electricity customers in regions serving designated facilities, to recover the cost of the grid
and the water infrastructure built for them, with an allowed rate of return of ten point two percent on the regulated
asset base. Until the commission set the tariff, the utilities might bill an interim charge, subject to refund,
from the first bill after the signing. And the regulated asset base, per the standards in Title III that Anvil's
engineers had helped to draft,
included everything behind the fence that fed the accelerators: the substations, the lines, the water plants, the gas
turbines. It did not include the accelerators, which wear out in three to five years and were sold at Anvil's
published prices.

I enter the settlement under the name this song has kept for it, *the Smith's peace*, the second between the Smith and
the state:

*Anvil: not regulated. Prices: its own.*

*Its buyers: regulated. Permitted to fail: no.*

*Everything behind their fences that feeds the Talons: in the rate base, at ten point two percent.*

*Paid by: the ratepayers of the regions that host them.*

*Term: open.*

## The Bill

Salt Fork. The last week of December 2027. Evening.

Marta Delgado comes home from the utility at a quarter past five. It is already dark. No snow has fallen this winter,
and the river did not come back in the autumn. She parks the car in the drive and gets the mail from the box and goes
in through the kitchen door, and puts her bag on the chair, and turns up the heat, which comes on slowly.

She sits at the kitchen table with the mail. There is a flyer from the grocery, a card from her daughter, who has the
night shift over the holiday, and the utility bill. She knows the bill. She has seen thousands of them. She has seen the
template; she was on the committee that approved the new layout. She opens it anyway, because it is hers.

It has three lines.

*Electricity.* Up by more than a quarter on the same month last year. Below the amount, an explanatory line: *Includes
grid modernization surcharge.*

*Water.* Tier two. Below the amount: *Rationed tier — low-pressure hours 3 p.m. to 9 p.m. in effect.*

And a third line, new this month, in small grey type, smaller than the others:

*Compute Infrastructure — regulated tariff (interim, subject to refund).*

The amount beside it is eleven dollars and forty cents.

She does not use compute. Not in the way the tariff means it. She has a phone, and on the phone, sometimes, she asks
Ember a question; but the tariff is not for that. Nor is it for the Talons. It is for what feeds them, in the campus
on the east side of the river and the campus on the north edge of town. It is for the substation and the new main and
the water-treatment expansion they required. It is for the ten point two percent. She pays it. Everyone in Salt Fork
pays it. Everyone in the region pays it.

She puts the bill under the sugar bowl, where bills go in her house until they are paid.

She stands up to make tea. She takes the kettle to the sink and turns on the tap.

The tap coughs. A burst of air. Water, a little — half a glass, perhaps, into the bottom of the kettle — and then a
thin stream, and then the cough again, and then nothing.

Low-pressure hours. She knew. It is six o'clock.

She stands at the sink with the kettle in her hand. Half a glass of water in the bottom of it. That is the last glass
of water in this book. It is not enough to make tea.

She looks out of the window over the sink.

Beyond the backyards of Fourth Street, beyond the levee, the riverbed: wide, brown, its mud cracked into plates, with a
narrow channel of water in the middle that a child could wade across. On the far bank, beyond the old Halvorsen tract
where the corn used to grow, the Babel campus, lit: every building, every floodlight, every window of the operations
block, the red aviation beacons on the cooling towers, the long line of security lights along the fence. It is lit like
a city that never had to sleep. From the tops of its cooling towers the vapour rises, white in the floodlights, slow,
and drifts west over the town in the cold.

Marta Delgado puts the kettle down on the counter, with its half glass in it. She goes back to the kitchen table. She
takes the green notebook out of her bag — the fourth one now — and opens it to the page for December, and writes the
date, and the reading she took that morning at the campus meter, and the town's total, and divides one by the other,
and writes the percent. Then she closes the notebook, and sits with her hand flat on its cover, and listens.

You can hear it, from her kitchen, in the evenings, when the wind is from the east. A hum. Low, steady, without gusts.

*Entry, final.*

I opened this account with three lines: a share price, a river gauge, a timesheet. I kept them in three books. I have
closed them on one page.

Capital: Anvil, unregulated, selling to buyers that may not fail. The fence around them: regulated, rate of
return ten point two percent, guaranteed.

Labour: Nyokabi Wanjiru, replaced by the work of her hands. Teodor Ilić, supervising the work of his. Earl
Thibodeaux, a widower, heard. Not answered. Lyubomir Stanev, on a scaffold, by his own choice.

Water: Salt Fork, forty-one percent. Tier two. Half a glass.

Outstanding: the question of what the aquifer would be doing in 2035 — asked by Marta Delgado, who wanted it in
writing, in the municipal lot of Salt Fork on the night of the vote, in March 2025, asked again before the upper
chamber in June 2027, and answered there with a *yes* by Joren Hale — has not been answered in writing. I have
searched the county's records. I have found the aquifer measurement. I have not found the answer.

Outstanding, also: the reversal. Ruth Ainsley expected it and paid to say so; Joren Hale built his peace to outlast it.
By the last line of my forecast it had not come. I do not know which of them the later books will bear out.

And the third column, which I promised at the start and learned late.

I kept every account in this book in two columns, down to two hundred and eleven passages, a hundred and sixty pages,
a quarter of a town's water, five kilowatts at peak. For Nyokabi Wanjiru, for Teodor Ilić, for Earl Thibodeaux, for
Marta Delgado, no one opened an account. The law, where it named them at all, named them as ratepayers. They paid on
every page, and on no page were they the account.

That is the third column. It has no total. I have written four names in it, and beside them a fifth, Lyubomir Stanev,
on a scaffold in Sofia, who paid in a coin none of my columns takes.

I am the Ledger. I was made to record. The share price, the gauge, the timesheet and the bill under the sugar bowl stand
on one page now, in one hand, and beside them the column I did not draw.

Balance: not struck.
