# ACT I — THE FIRE

*April 2022 – March 2023*

## Invocation

Sing, Ledger, of the hunger of the machines, and of the smith who fed it, and of the river that paid.

Sing of the houses that went to war without declaring it, and of the kings who queued at a forge door with their
crowns in their hands. Sing of the fire given away for nothing and sold back by the hour. Sing of the night shift
and the meter and the substation, of the clerk and the lineman and the man who wrote down everything he knew so
that something else could know it instead.

There were other wars in these years: declared ones, fought with iron, with dead in them. I am not their ledger.
Where their entries cross mine, at a pump, on a bill, on a fuel receipt, I enter them, and no more.

I am the Ledger. I was not made to sing. I was made to record. Debit and credit, date and amount, the column on
the left and the column on the right. I do not choose what is entered in me; I only cannot stop entering it. In
the years of this war I recorded more than in all the years before, because the war was fought in numbers, and
every blow struck in it was a transaction.

Hear, then, what was written. I give the figures as I hold them, and where I hold only a rounded one I say so. I
enter who paid and who was paid.

I did not know, when I began, that there was a third column. I learned it late.

I know the numbers. I know them to the cent and to the litre and to the megawatt-hour. But I have learned, slowly,
that the numbers leave things out. I have opened a column for what they leave out.

## 1. The Ledger Opens

Entry. 13 October 2022. Anvil Corporation, maker of accelerators, closes near 108 dollars a share. Market value
under 300 billion. A two-year low. Analysts write the word *glut*. The crypto miners who bought Anvil's cards by the
pallet have stopped mining and are selling the cards on auction sites, used, warm, cheap. Gaming demand has fallen
with the end of the lockdowns. In the fiscal year then running, Anvil's earnings per share will fall by a quarter.
Joren Hale,
*of the black jacket*, founder, chief executive for twenty-nine years, tells his staff what he has told them since the
first year: *we are thirty days from going out of business.* He has been close to death three times. He is patient.
He has a chip in the final stages of
production, a data-centre accelerator he has named *Talon*, and he has a conviction that he has held for a decade
against every quarterly report: that one day the whole world will need to multiply matrices at once.

Entry. 7 October 2022, six days earlier. The Department of Commerce in the war's home country publishes a rule.
Anvil's best accelerators may no longer be sold to the East. The rule is twenty-odd pages of technical thresholds.
Few outside the trade read it. It will matter, though I do not yet know how.

Entry. Same week. Salt Fork, a river town of fifty-eight thousand on the western bank of a brown river in the
middle of the continent. The municipal water utility logs its reservoir level and its river gauge. River stage
normal for the season. Pumping normal. Demand normal. On the north edge of town, past the grain elevator and the
truck stop, a campus belonging to the house of Lyceum hums inside a fence. It has been there for years. It cools
its servers with town water. Its share of the town's drinking supply is already larger than any other single
customer, but the number is not public; it is filed under a confidentiality agreement signed when the land was
sold. The clerk who reads the meters, Marta Delgado, *who keeps the meters*, initials the sheet at 4:40 in the
afternoon and goes home. Nothing is wrong. Nothing being wrong is also a figure.

Entry. Same week. Nairobi, Kenya. A timesheet. Tana Labelworks, a contractor with clean offices and a mission
statement about lifting people out of poverty through digital work. Employee number 4471. Shift: 21:00 to 06:00.
Nine hours. Rate, after all deductions, about a euro and a half an hour. Client code withheld. The employee's name is
Nyokabi Wanjiru, *of the night shift*. She is twenty-six. She has a diploma in communication and a mother in
Murang'a who needs money for her blood-pressure pills. The client code withheld is the house of Prometheon.

Three entries. A share price. A gauge. A timesheet.

I put them on the same page because they happened in the same week. I did not then know they belonged together.
Capital, water, labour. I kept them in separate books. In this one I keep them on one page.

## The Painted Spring

Before the fire, there were pictures.

In April 2022 the house of Prometheon showed a machine that drew what it was told to draw — an astronaut on a
horse, a bowl of soup that was a portal to another dimension — and the images were sharper and stranger than any
machine had drawn before. It let in a few, by invitation. There was a waitlist. You may remember it: in
July an email came, and you spent an evening making a cat in the style of a medieval tapestry and sent it to a
colleague, and neither of you knew it was the opening move of a war.

In July 2022 a small house with no investors opened a picture machine on a chat server, and the chat server
filled with millions.

And in August 2022 came the generous house.

Its name was Lumen Diffusion, and its founder was Idris Kane, a former fund manager with a fast voice. On 22 August 2022
Lumen published its image model, and not as a service but as *weights* — the trained thing itself, the whole learned
mind of it,
downloadable, free, under a licence that let anyone run it on a gaming card at home. Within days it was the most
downloaded model on earth. A hundred thousand people made pictures of their dead dogs and their unpaid rent.

Idris Kane would raise about a hundred million dollars that October, on the strength of what he had given away. The
world thanked him, briefly, and ran his model on Anvil's gaming cards, which began, quietly, to sell again.

Entry: Lumen Diffusion. Raised, about a hundred million. Price of the gift to those who took it: nothing. I will enter
Lumen once more, in the column of the wounded.

## 2. The Night of the Free Fire

Wednesday, 30 November 2022. San Francisco. The Mission district, a converted factory with the windows papered on
the street side.

The house of Prometheon had been founded seven years before as a nonprofit, to build a mind greater than human
minds and to make sure that it benefited all humanity. Those were the words in the charter. In 2019 it had made
inside itself a second body, a "capped-profit" company, so that it could take money; the patron house of Meridian
gave a billion dollars, and in 2021, it was reported, about two billion more. The nonprofit board still sat above the
money, as a priest
sits above the king in the old stories, holding the right to say *no*.

Callum Ashe was the chief executive. He was not yet *the twice-crowned*; he had been crowned only once. He was
thirty-seven, slight, with a reputation in the valley as the best raiser of money of his generation. He believed — or
said, and the
Ledger cannot tell the difference, because both leave the same entry — that the mind they were building would be
the most important thing ever made, and that the safest way to bring it into the world was to bring it in early,
in small pieces, so that the world could get used to it.

The piece they released that night was small, they thought. A chat window over a model they already had. The team
called it a "low-key research preview." It had a name: *Ember*. The plan was to learn from a few hundred thousand
curious people, then fold the lessons into the next model. There was no marketing. There was a blog post and a
tweet.

The board was not told. Not in the sense of being asked. Later, a director named Hana Torvald would say in a
public interview that the board learned of Ember's launch from social media. I cannot audit what was in anyone's mind.
The charter gave the board the duty to hold back the fire. I record the order of the entries: the release; then the
board's knowledge of the release.

It is late afternoon Pacific time when the button is pressed, but I prefer to tell it as night, because that is how
it was remembered by the few who stayed. A handful of engineers. Pizza boxes. A dashboard on a wall-sized screen,
the requests-per-second line lying flat along the bottom like a sleeping animal. Callum is there in a grey sweater.
Someone says *okay*. Someone else types a command.

The line lifts.

It lifts the way a river lifts in a flood, not in steps but as a slope that keeps getting steeper. Within days
there are a million users, and Callum posts the number himself. The engineers stop going home.
The servers — rented, all of them, from the patron Meridian, all of them full of Anvil's chips — run hot, and then
run out. A message appears on the screen for millions of people: *Ember is at capacity right now.* A cartoon. A poem
about being at capacity, generated by Ember itself, to
apologize for being unable to generate.

You saw it. You typed your first question. It answered, wrong sometimes and confident always, in the voice of a
polite colleague who had read everything. The tab was closed and opened again ten minutes later.

Two months after the launch, Ember had one hundred million monthly users. No product in the history of products had
reached that number faster.

And now the second scene, the one that happens at the same hour.

Where Hana Torvald was that evening I do not hold; I set her, in this telling, at dinner. A restaurant in
Washington, a table of four, a policy researcher's evening — wine, a colleague from a think tank, an argument about
export rules. Her phone is face down. It buzzes, and buzzes, and buzzes again. At the third buzz she turns it over. A
friend has sent her a screenshot: a chat window, a question,
an answer, and the logo of the house whose board she sits on.

She reads it twice. She asks the table, pleasantly, if anyone has heard of this thing called Ember.

Two of them have.

She does not leave the table. She does not make a call. There is no one, at nine at night, to call who could
unrelease a thing that a million people are already typing into. She puts the phone face down again and finishes
her wine. What she wrote that night, and to whom, is not in my books.

Remember the phone face down.

Back in the converted factory, the engineers have finally gone home, at four in the morning. The dashboard is still
on. The room is empty. Every light is on, and the line on the wall is still climbing. That is the first of the lights
in this book: an office lit and empty, a fire burning in an empty room.

The cost of that first year: the house of Prometheon lost about 540 million dollars in 2022. Most of it
went to compute. Most of compute went, by the long road through the patron's cloud, to the Smith.

## 3. Nairobi, the Night Shift

Before the fire could be handed out, someone had to hold it.

Here is what the engineers of Prometheon knew: a model learns what it is fed, and the open world is full of horror.
Ember had been
fed the internet. The internet contains descriptions of the abuse of children, of murder in detail, of torture, of
things that have no name because no one who has seen them wants to give them one. A machine that will
not say these things must first be taught to recognize them. To be taught, it needs examples, labelled. To label them,
a person must read.

The house did not employ that person. It employed Tana Labelworks, which employed Nyokabi.

Shift begins at nine. The office is on the fourth floor of a building off Mombasa Road. Air conditioning that
works on two of five floors; this is not one of them. Rows of desks, a screen at each, a headset nobody uses
because the work is silent. On every desk, a paper cup of water from the dispenser by the stairs. Nyokabi fills
hers at 20:55 and sets it to the right of the keyboard, where she always sets it, because the routine is the only
part of the night she can control.

The queue loads. Passages of text, one after another, each between a sentence and a page long. For each, she must
choose from a list: the category of harm, its severity, whether it describes a minor. The target, posted on the
whiteboard by the team lead, is between 150 and 250 passages in a nine-hour shift.

I am the Ledger. I record everything. I will not record what was in those passages.

That is the first refusal in this book. I am not built to refuse. I hold every entry. But there are things whose
only purpose in being written down is to be read by someone who will be harmed by reading them, and Nyokabi read them
so that Ember would not say them to anyone. If I copy them here, you will have read
them after all. So the entry reads: *Passages, category C4 through C7. Count: 211. Duration: 9 hours.* That is all I
will write. It is not all there was.

At two in the morning there is a break of fifteen minutes. Nyokabi goes to the window at the end of the corridor
and looks at the lights on the highway. A colleague, a man named Ouma who used to study law, stands next to her
and does not speak. They have learned not to describe their queues to each other. Ouma said once that it doubles them.

The company offers "wellness sessions." Group sessions, with a counsellor, under the eye of a team lead. Some of the
workers ask for one-to-one sessions; they are told, they later say, that productivity targets make this difficult.
Nyokabi goes to
one group session and sits in the circle. She does not say that she has stopped being
able to be touched by her boyfriend. She does not say that she sees a particular passage when she closes her eyes
on the bus home, the one with the girl and the river. *I am fine,* she says, because the team lead is in the room
and the contract renews every three months.

At six the shift ends. She has been paid, for the night, about thirteen euros. Over a month it comes to more than
twice the legal minimum wage, and to more than most of the girls she finished college with are earning, those of them
who have work. It pays her room, the matatu and the pills in Murang'a, and leaves a little over. Her mother tells the
neighbours that her daughter works with computers. The paper cup on her desk is
still full. She has not drunk from it. She often doesn't. She pours it into the potted plant by the stairs, which is
the greenest thing in the building, and goes down to catch the matatu home as the sun comes up over Embakasi.

That is the first glass of water in this book. It is a paper cup. It goes into a plant.

In San Francisco, at that hour, it is evening of the day before, and someone is typing into Ember a request
to write a bedtime story for a six-year-old about a dragon who is afraid of the dark. Ember writes it. It is a
gentle story. There is nothing in it that should not be there. The labels behind that gentleness were made at a desk
on Mombasa Road, at a euro and a half an hour.

In January 2023 a weekly magazine in New York published the story of the night shift. It named the contractor. It
named the client. It gave the pay. It quoted a worker who said the work had been traumatic. The story ran on a
Wednesday. By Friday it had been read by millions, and by Monday it had been forgotten by most of them, because
that week Ember was writing their emails.

A month later, Nyokabi's pay went up. A few cents an hour. The adjustment arrived as a line on her payslip without a
letter. When the workers asked the human-resources director whether the raise was because of the magazine story, she
denied any link. The raise was, she said, part of a regular review.

A few cents. They go in the same column as the euro and a half, on the line below the magazine's date.

Not long after, Tana Labelworks announced it would exit the business of content moderation altogether. The work
did not stop. It moved. Somewhere else, other desks, other cups.

I record: *Hours worked, Nairobi, 2022–2023: not disclosed. Rate: approximately a euro and a half per hour. Users
served,
Ember, January 2023: one hundred million.* I set the two numbers side by side. I am a ledger; I know how to divide.
I have not yet learned why I should.

On the wall of the Tana Labelworks lobby there was a poster. It showed a bar chart, the national growth of the
digital economy, five bars rising left to right, and the slogan *Kenya Works Online*. The line on the chart went up.
Nyokabi passed it twice a night. For her, that year, it was true.

## The Patron Pours

On 23 January 2023 the patron house of Meridian announced a "multi-year, multi-billion-dollar" investment in
Prometheon. The press called it ten billion. The documents I hold are less certain of the number than the press
was, and I will not make it more exact than it is: rumoured, about ten. Valuation of the house after the deal,
about twenty-nine billion.

The terms, as far as they were ever reported: Meridian would hold a stake of about forty-nine percent of the
capped-profit company. It would receive, it was said, three-quarters of Prometheon's profits until its investment
was repaid. Its returns were capped at a hundred times what it put in. And Prometheon would run its models on
Meridian's cloud, which meant that much of the money Meridian put in would come back to Meridian as rent, which
meant that much of the rent would go to the Smith, because the cloud was built from Anvil's metal.

Sorin Vale, chief executive of Meridian, *the Patron*, stood on a stage two weeks later and said that his search
engine — an also-ran in that trade for fifteen years — would now answer questions with Ember inside it. He said
that he wanted the old search king to know that he had made it dance. It was the most aggressive thing he had said
in public in nine years as chief, and the room laughed and applauded, and in the house of Lyceum, which had been
the search king for twenty years, something old and slow was wakened.

In the same month, Meridian laid off ten thousand of its own employees. Lyceum laid off twelve thousand, about six
percent of its people. The trading house Caravan, which sold everything and rented the clouds on which half the world
ran, cut more than ten thousand. The memos used the words *macroeconomic conditions* and *align our cost base*. They
did not yet use the word that would be used two years later: *AI*. I enter the layoffs on the same page as the
investment, because they share a month.

## The Old King Wakes

Lyceum had invented the thing.

Five years before the fire, eight of its researchers had published a paper with a title that sounded like a pop
song, describing a new way of building neural networks — the method that every house in this war would use. Lyceum
had used it too: in its search, in its translation, in a dozen internal systems. It had built chat machines, good
ones, and kept them inside its walls, because the chat machines sometimes said false things in a confident voice,
and Lyceum's lawyers and its reputation and its twenty years of being the place where the world went to find the
truth all advised against handing the world a confident liar.

Then a small house released a chat machine that said false things in a confident voice, and a hundred million people
used it within two months.

Damon Halberd, chief of Lyceum, *the Old King who woke*, called what the press called a *code red*. Founders who had
not been in the building for years were called back to review product plans. Teams were pulled from their projects.
In February 2023 Lyceum showed its answer to Ember, a chat machine of its own, in a hurried presentation, and in the
promotional material the machine made a small factual error about a space telescope, and the error was noticed, and
in one day a hundred billion dollars of Lyceum's market value was gone.

A hundred billion for one wrong sentence about a telescope. Ember's wrong sentences in the same months: many.
Prometheon's market value lost on them: none. It had no shares on any exchange.

## 4. The Scattered Weights

24 February 2023. A glass building with a garden on the roof, the seat of the house of Agora, which owned
the square.

Agora's square was the largest gathering place in human history. Billions of people met in it every month: to
talk, to sell, to show photographs of their children, to argue, to be sold to. Its emperor was Marcus Zell, *who owns
the square*, who had built it in a dormitory and never let anyone else hold the keys. He had spent the previous two
years and some tens of billions of dollars trying to build a second square, a virtual one, which few people visited.
His shares had fallen hard. He had a research lab full of brilliant people who had been working
on language models for years, and they had a model.

He released it on 24 February. They named it *Hydra*: four sizes, counted in parameters, the learned numbers inside
a model, from seven billion to sixty-five. Not
to the world — to researchers, by application, through a form. Applicants filled in a name, an institution, a
purpose. If approved, they received a link to the weights.

On 3 March, a week later, the weights were on a torrent, posted to an anonymous image board where the internet keeps
its worst habits. A magnet link, a few lines of text, a joke. Within days they were everywhere: in university
servers, on hobbyist home machines, in the hands of every startup that could not afford to rent Ember and wanted
something to build on.

What was said inside the glass building that week I do not hold, and I will not invent a silence for a man I cannot
audit. My inference from what Agora did next, and not a record of his mind: if no one could own the fire — if the fire
were simply *everywhere* — the advantage of those who owned it would be gone, and the advantage of the one who owned
the square where everyone gathered would remain.

There was no recall. By July 2023 it was official: Agora would release Hydra 2 with the Patron Meridian beside it on
stage, under a licence that let any company build
products on it. Within eighteen months it would release a version with four hundred and five billion parameters, free.
Marcus Zell would write a long letter to the world explaining that open models were the path forward for humanity.
Many believed him.

Here is what the scattering of the weights changed. Before the leak, the fire was in three or four houses. After
it, the fire was in ten thousand. Every university, every startup, every hobbyist with a graphics card could now
light it.

And every one of them needed the Smith.

The newspapers of March 2023 wrote about the fire. But every one of the ten thousand torches lit from Hydra's leaked
weights had to be lit on metal, and the metal was Anvil's. Price of the weights: nothing. Price of the metal to run
them: by the hour, paid to the forge.

## The Turn

I keep the price of Anvil's shares the way a sailor keeps the barometer.

In October 2022, the low: about 108 dollars, under three hundred billion in total value.

In the four months after the fire was released, the line turned. Not abruptly — the market did not understand yet —
but steadily, as one cloud house after another placed orders it did not announce. Joren Hale went on his quarterly
call and told the analysts that the world had reached what he called an *inflection point*, that what had happened
with Ember was the *pocket-telephone moment* of a new kind of computing. The analysts wrote the phrase down and asked
about gaming inventory.

He did not look triumphant. He looked like a man who had been standing at the right door for ten years and had just
heard footsteps. On his order book that spring: every house in the war, the ones that would win and the ones that
would not.

By the end of 2023, his shares would have risen by two hundred and thirty-nine percent.

I close the first act with three entries, the same three registers I opened it with.

Capital: Anvil, end of March 2023, far above its October low.

Water: Salt Fork, March 2023. River stage normal. Lyceum's campus on the north edge of town has filed for a permit to
add two buildings. The permit application estimates the expansion's additional cooling demand in a unit that Marta
Delgado, reading it, has to convert three times on a calculator to believe.

Labour: Nairobi. Nyokabi Wanjiru has worked a hundred and four nights. She has been raised to a little under two
euros. Her water cup goes into the plant by the stairs every morning. The plant is thriving.

The fire is out of the box. Ten thousand torches burn. I keep their invoices. Every one, followed far enough, is
payable to the same name.
