# ACT IV — THE TOWER

*January 2025 – autumn 2026*

## Invocation

Sing, Ledger, of the war when it came down out of the cloud and stood on the ground.

For two years it had been fought in a place no one could visit: in weights and gradients and tokens, in rounds of
funding and in benchmark tables, in the air above the world. Now it put its feet on farmland. It needed acres. It
needed gigawatts. It needed rivers. Sing of the Tower that was measured not in storeys but in power, and of the
towns that were asked to lend it their water and their wire.

Sing of the prices in this act: the price of an answer from a mind, falling; the price of a megawatt-hour, of a tonne
of copper, of an acre beside a river, rising; the price of an engineer's hour, falling.

In this act I began to enter, beside each number, the place on the ground where it was paid.

## 12. The Tower of Babel

Tuesday, 21 January 2025. The palace. The room with the portraits and the long table, where presidents receive
foreign leaders and announce wars.

Three men stand behind a lectern beside the new President.

Callum Ashe, *the twice-crowned*, in a dark suit, no tie.

Ren Okabe, founder of the Horizon Fund, the largest technology investor in the world, a man who had once lost
seventy billion dollars in a single year and said it was the price of seeing the future early; a man who had been
promising for a decade that a superintelligence would arrive within it.

Harlan Pike, chairman of Delphi Data, the old database house that had come late to the cloud and now sold more
capacity than it could build; a man of eighty, tanned, a sailor, one of the richest men alive.

The President reads the figure. *Five hundred billion dollars*. Over four years. To build the forges of the war on
the soil of the home country. A hundred billion of it, the partners say, would be deployed *immediately*. The joint
venture has a name. I call it *Babel*, after the old tower that was built to reach a height never reached.

Harlan Pike says that the first site is already under construction: Caldera, Texas, on the plains, where ten
buildings are going up already. Callum Ashe says that this will be *the most important project of this era*. Ren
Okabe says that the age of golden prosperity is beginning. The President says it is the largest infrastructure
project in history, and that it will create a hundred thousand jobs, *almost immediately*.

On the lectern, beside the President's hand, there is a small bottle of water, a plastic one, with the cap still on.
On the table behind them there are four more, one for each man. They are cold; condensation runs down them under
the lights of the cameras. None is opened during the twenty-two minutes of the announcement. At the end, an aide
gathers them up, unopened, and puts them back in a refrigerator in a pantry off the corridor.

Not said at the podium: *gigawatts*; where the power would come from; where the water would come from. A reporter
asked; the President answered that they would build their own power plants, right on
site, and that he would make it easy for them with emergency declarations. Asked how much of the five hundred
billion was actually committed, Pike smiled and said they had the money. Tycho Strand, who was not at the podium
and who had recently been very close to the President, posted that night that the partners *don't actually have the
money*. Callum Ashe replied that Strand was wrong, and invited him to visit the site.

The figures: pledged, five hundred billion; immediate, one hundred billion; in the bank on the day, an amount none
of the partners disclosed.

Now cut.

**Salt Fork. Tuesday, 18 March 2025. Seven in the evening.**

The council chamber of Salt Fork is a room in the basement of the municipal building, with a low ceiling, a flag,
a dais of seven seats, and seventy folding chairs, steel, the kind that pinch the fingers when they are folded. On
an ordinary night, eleven people attend. Tonight all seventy are taken and people stand along the walls, under the
fluorescent lights, in their work clothes.

The item on the agenda is number nine: *Ordinance 2025-14, approving a development agreement and water-service
agreement with Prairie Compute Holdings LLC for a data center campus on the former Halvorsen tract*.

Prairie Compute Holdings LLC is a name on a form. Marta Delgado knows what it means, because she was in the room in
February when the engineers came to talk to the utility about the main they would need. It is one of the forges of the
Tower. It will be built on four hundred acres of corn on the east side of
the river, and it will be cooled, in its first phase, by town water, and later, they say, by a closed-loop system
that will use much less. They say *later*.

Marta does not usually speak at council. She is a clerk. Her job is to read the meters, not to have opinions about
them. But she has the green notebook, now two years thick, and she has asked her supervisor, and her supervisor has
said that she can speak as a resident, not as a utility employee, and she has written down what she will say on an
index card.

The developer's representative speaks first: a woman in a navy suit with a slide deck. The campus will cost four
billion dollars. It will bring nine hundred construction jobs for three years. It will bring sixty permanent jobs.
It will pay, under a tax agreement with the county, about two million dollars a year in lieu of property taxes for
the first fifteen years. It will fund a new fire truck and a new scoreboard for the high school football field.
There is a rendering on the screen: low white buildings, green berms, a pond with a fountain, trees that do not yet
exist.

Then the public comment. The president of the chamber of commerce. A carpenter from the union hall. A farmer who
sold the land, who says it was his to sell. A retired schoolteacher who says she is worried about the river. A young
man who says his father worked at the meatpacker and lost his job when it automated and now there will finally be
something. A pastor. A woman who runs the diner by the highway and says the construction crews will save her
business.

Marta is called eleventh. She walks to the microphone. Her hands are cold. She looks at the index card and then puts
it down, because the card has the numbers and she knows the numbers.

She says: *My name is Marta Delgado, I live on Fourth Street, and I've read water meters in this town for
twenty-one years.* She says that she cannot share the exact figures from the utility because of a confidentiality
agreement. She says that the campus on the north side, which has been here for years, which is good for the town in
many ways, already uses about a quarter of the drinking water this town treats. A quarter. She says that she does not
know how much the new campus will use, because the agreement on the table doesn't say. It says *up to*. She says the
river was the lowest last August it has been since she started. She says she isn't against jobs. She says she would
just like somebody to tell her what the number is.

She sits down. There is some applause, not much. The developer's representative is asked by a councilman to respond.
She says that the company is committed to being a good steward of local resources, that the closed-loop system will
dramatically reduce water use after the first phase, that detailed figures are commercially sensitive but have
been shared with the utility under the agreement. Marta, in her folding chair, hears this and understands that she,
the utility, will soon hold a second set of numbers she cannot say out loud.

The vote is called at 10:42 p.m. The clerk reads the names.

Aye. Aye. Nay. Aye. Nay. Nay. Aye.

Four to three. The ordinance passes. Every councilman who voted aye, when he explained his vote, used the word
*jobs*. Two of the nays said *water*. One of the nays said that he did not like the tax agreement.

People fold the chairs. The steel snaps. The janitor stacks them on a cart. In the corridor, the developer's
representative shakes hands with the mayor.

In the municipal lot, a man from the county's economic development office put his hand on Marta's shoulder and told
her not to take it personally. She said she did not. She said she only wanted to know, in writing, what the aquifer
would be doing in 2035. He said that was a good question. She asked him to put it in writing. He said he would. He
never did. I have searched the county's records for the years since and have not found the answer.

Marta puts on her coat and walks home along Fourth Street under the streetlights, and when she gets home she takes out
the green notebook and turns to a new page and writes, at the top, in capitals: *BABEL — SALT FORK*. Below it, she
writes the date. She does not have a number to write under it yet.

On the left, in the palace: five hundred billion dollars. On the right, in a basement with folding chairs: a woman who
would like somebody to tell her what the number is.

I had kept them in different books. That night I opened them on the same desk, and under the Tower's pledge I wrote a
new line: *Salt Fork, water, Prairie Compute: up to —.* I had never before written in the right-hand column. I began,
that night, to write in it.

## The Unbinding

Two days after the Tower: Thursday, 23 January 2025.

The new President signed another order, titled *Removing Barriers to American Leadership in Artificial Intelligence*.
The removal of rules would be the strategy.

In February, Covenant released *Vigil Hand*: an agent that lived in the terminal of a programmer's computer and could
read a whole codebase, plan a change, edit the files, run the tests, read the errors, and try again. Not an
assistant. A pair of hands. Within months, engineers everywhere used it. They wrote to each other that it was the
first time a mind had felt like a colleague rather than a tool. They said it the way people talk about a new hire who
is better than they expected. Some said it the way people talk about a new hire who is better than *they* are.

In May 2025, under pressure from the attorneys-general of two states, from former employees and from Tycho
Strand's lawsuit, Prometheon announced that its nonprofit would keep control of the company, even as the company
became an ordinary one, a public benefit corporation, able to raise larger sums. The priest kept his seat above the
king.

And the trade had a new word for what it was doing. The word was *capex*. Capital expenditure. When a house raised its
capex, its stock rose.

## 13. The Efficiency Letter

Columbus, Ohio. A Wednesday in June 2025.

Teodor Ilić is forty-seven. He has used Vigil Hand every day since March. It has changed his work in a way he finds
difficult to describe to Jelena. He no longer writes most of the code. He describes what he wants and reviews what
the agent produces. He catches its mistakes, which are fewer each month. He explains to it the parts of the routing
engine that are not in the code: why the customs module has a special case for one port in Belgium; why the
warehouse-slotting algorithm must never be run during the last hour of a shift; why the third column of a certain
table is called `legacy_flag_2` and must never be deleted, because a single customer in Mexico depends on it in a
way no one ever documented.

On 2 April the President announced duties on the goods of nearly every country that traded with the home country. On
the ninth a pause of ninety days was declared, and two days later chips were exempted. In those weeks the tables in
Kestrel's customs module changed so often that Teodor rewrote its
rate file some days twice. The agent watched how he did it.

In the same April, the company launched a project called *Agent Readiness*. Each engineering team was asked to
prepare its
systems for *AI-native operations*. In practice, this meant writing documentation. Not for humans: for agents.
Structured descriptions of each service: what it did, what it depended on, what could go wrong, what to do when it
did. Runbooks. Decision trees. The tacit knowledge, written down. A template was provided. It had a field called
*Context the agent needs that is not in the code*.

Teodor was assigned the routing engine. He had written most of it.

You know this assignment. It came, perhaps, as a ticket: *Document service X for agent onboarding*. Perhaps it was
called something else — *knowledge capture*, *bus-factor reduction*, *platform readiness*. You did it carefully,
because it is good engineering practice to document a system, and because no one should be the only person who knows
why `legacy_flag_2` exists.

It took him eleven weeks. He wrote a hundred and sixty pages. He wrote down the case of the Belgian port and the
reason for the last hour of the shift and the Mexican customer. He wrote down the three times in eleven years that
the database had corrupted and how he had fixed it each time. He wrote down things he had never told anyone, because
no one had ever asked; things that were in his head and nowhere else. It had a section headed *Things that will
look wrong and are not*. He was, he told Jelena, oddly proud of it. It
was the best documentation he had ever written. The agent, given the documentation, could now handle most of what
he handled. He tested it himself. He gave it the three historical corruptions as exercises. It solved two of them.

On a Tuesday afternoon he marked the ticket *Done*. His manager reacted to the ticket with a thumbs-up emoji.

On Tuesday evening, at 9:14 p.m., a calendar invitation appeared in his inbox. Thursday, 10:00 a.m. Thirty
minutes. Organizer: his manager's manager. Second attendee: a name he did not know, with the title *People Partner*.
Subject: *Quick sync*. No agenda.

He did not sleep well. On Wednesday he worked normally, or tried to. A colleague in Rotterdam messaged him at noon
his time — the colleague had received the same invitation, for Thursday, at 4:00 p.m. Rotterdam time. They did not
say anything more about it.

On Thursday at 10:00 a.m. he joined the video call from his kitchen. His manager's manager read from a document. He
read rather than spoke, so that each word would be the word that had been approved. Kestrel Systems was *taking
decisive action to position the company for an AI-first future*. The company was *reallocating investment toward AI
capabilities and infrastructure*. As part of this, certain roles were being *eliminated*. Teodor's role was among
them. This was *not a reflection of his performance*, which had been *exceptional*. His access would end at noon. He
would receive twelve weeks of severance, plus one week for every year of service, plus continuation of health
insurance for three months. The People Partner would send the details. Was there anything he wanted to ask?

He asked one question. He asked who would maintain the routing engine.

His manager's manager said that the platform team would *own it going forward, with agentic support*.

The People Partner said: *Your document is very thorough. The agents will be grateful for it.*

The call lasted fourteen minutes. At noon his laptop logged him out. The message on the screen said that his
account had been disabled, and to contact IT for assistance.

The company that week cut about eleven percent of its workforce. The press release said *efficiency*. It said
*AI*. The local business journal ran it on page three.

In that year and the next, the great houses cut too. Caravan cut about fourteen thousand corporate jobs, about four
percent of its office staff; its people chief tied the cuts to the new technology, and its chief executive said a
week later it was about culture. Meridian, *the Patron*, cut about nine thousand, *to redirect spending toward AI and
cloud*.

The firm that counts the layoffs of the home country keeps a column for the reason the employer gives. In 2025 the
reason given was the minds for one cut in twenty. In the first months of 2026 it would be the minds for more than one
in five. Some economists wrote that the reason given is not always the reason. I copy the column as it was kept.

That afternoon, Teodor drove. He did not want to sit in the kitchen. He drove east out of the city on the state
route, through the outer suburbs, past the fields, towards a town called Galena Crossing where Jelena's school was,
because he thought he might pick her up.

Before Galena Crossing, where there had been a soybean field when they moved to Ohio, there was now a construction
site the size of a village. Cranes. Trucks. Rows of concrete pads. A sign at the entrance: the name of a holding
company, the name of an electric utility, the words *Substation Expansion Project — Powering Ohio's Future*. Behind
the substation site, in the distance, long windowless buildings the colour of sand, three of them finished, four more
rising. A data-centre campus. He did not know whose. It did not say.

He pulled over onto the shoulder. He sat in the car with the engine running and the air conditioning on and looked at
the substation for a long time. A line of steel lattice towers marched away from it across the fields towards the
horizon, new and silver, carrying power in.

He did the arithmetic. He could not help it; he was an engineer. His salary had been a hundred and sixty-four thousand
dollars a year. That was, at about two thousand hours, about eighty-two dollars an hour — about seventy-five euros. A
Talon in the cloud had cost seven dollars fifty an hour two years before. Now, used, on the spot market, it cost
between a dollar twenty-five and two dollars thirty-five an hour. A Talon could run the agent that would do his job.
A full box of eight cost, together, less than nineteen dollars an hour: less than a quarter of his.

He thought: *I wrote it down for them. I wrote down everything.* He thought of the hundred and sixty pages, and the
Belgian port, and `legacy_flag_2`. He had been asked to write it down, and he had written it well.

He did not pick up Jelena. He drove home. When she came in that evening and saw his face she knew before he said
anything, and she sat down at the kitchen table beside him and put her hand over his, and they did not speak for a
while. Then he said it had been a day.

On the kitchen radio, low, the news: the economy had grown faster than expected in the second quarter, led by
business investment in information-processing equipment, which economists attributed to the boom in artificial
intelligence. Without it, said one economist, growth would have been far weaker.

Neither of them heard it.

I record: *Teodor Ilić. Hours of documentation, Agent Readiness project: about four hundred and forty. Cost to the
company: about thirty-six thousand dollars.* Next entry, two days later: *role eliminated.*

## Murang'a, a Sunday

August 2025. The hills north of Nairobi, a house with a tin roof and a kitchen garden.

Nyokabi Wanjiru's mother has fallen twice since the long rains: once in the garden among the kale, once on the step
of the latrine. She says it is her age. She says the sun. She is sixty-three and has taken a pill for her blood pressure
for six years, and lately she has been dizzy in the mornings, and she has not told her daughter, because her daughter
sends the money for the pills and has enough to carry.

Nyokabi finds out from a neighbour. On the Sunday she takes the matatu up from the city and sits at her mother's table
and asks to see the pills.

There are two packets. One comes from the county dispensary, when the dispensary has stock. The other comes from the
chemist in town, bought when the dispensary ran out in the spring, with a different maker's name and a different
colour on the box. The nurse at the dispensary had said: take your pressure pill every morning. The chemist had said
the same. Her mother has been taking one from each box, every morning, since April.

Nyokabi photographs the two packets on the table, front and back, with her phone, and asks Ember in English whether
they are the same medicine.

The answer comes in a few seconds. It says that both contain the same
substance at the same strength, under two makers' names; that taking one of each would double the dose; that a double
dose of this kind of medicine can lower the blood pressure too far and cause dizziness and falls, most of all in older
people and in the mornings. It says not to stop or change the medicine without a clinician, and to take both boxes to
the clinic as soon as possible. It is plain, and it does not hedge where it does not need to.

That evening, waiting for the matatu back, her mother asks for the first time what the work is that pays for the
pills. Nyokabi tells her the part that can be told: that she reads for a computer, so that it learns which words are
bad. Her mother thinks about it. *That is a priest's work,* she says, *not a girl's.* Nyokabi does not answer, and her
mother does not ask again.

On Monday Nyokabi calls the dispensary from the bus. The clinical officer listens, asks the names on the boxes, and
says: one box, one pill, and come in on Thursday. On Thursday her mother's pressure is measured, the extra box is taken
away, and the dose is written on a card in large letters.

Within the week the dizziness stops. By September her mother is back in the garden before breakfast. Half of what
Nyokabi had been sending for pills each month had been buying the second box. She sends the same amount, and her mother
buys flour with the rest.

I enter it on its own line, in the same hand as everything else in this book: *Murang'a. A double dose found from a
photograph, on a Sunday, at no charge. Confirmed by the clinic. Falls since: none recorded.* It is true. I set nothing
beside it.

## 14. Ice Night in Ashby Parish

January 2026. Northeast Louisiana.

The storm came in from the west on a Tuesday night as rain, and the temperature fell through the night, and by
Wednesday morning the rain was freezing as it touched anything. It coated every branch, every fence wire, every
power line, in a clear sleeve of ice that thickened by the hour. The weather service called it a significant ice
event. The people in the parish called it, afterwards, the icepocalypse.

Earl Thibodeaux had seen many ice storms in thirty-four years on the poles. He knew what a quarter inch of ice did to
a line, and what half an inch did, and what an inch did. By Wednesday noon there was close to an inch.

At 2:17 on Wednesday afternoon, the power in the brick house on the gravel lane went out.

Across the road, the campus of Agora was half finished, and the half that was finished was running. Earl could see it
from his kitchen window: the long low buildings, the fence, the floodlights on poles at the corners, the windows of
the operations building. When the power went out in his house, he looked out of the window, the way everyone does,
to see whether the neighbours' lights had gone too.

The campus was lit.

Every floodlight. Every window. The beacon on top of the cooling plant. The security lights along the fence, every
fifty feet, in a perfect line that ran away from him into the grey afternoon. The rest of the parish — the road, the
other houses on the lane, the church on the highway — was dark.

Lorraine came to stand beside him.

*That's them,* Earl said. His wife asked who. *The ones on the field,* he said. *They'll have their own generators.
And their own line from the new plant.*

A data centre of that size would have its own substation, its own feed from the new gas plants the utility had built,
and banks of diesel generators for exactly this. He knew that. He had been a lineman.

But he also knew the substation two miles down the road, the one he had helped rebuild in 1998. He knew what it had
been built to carry. And he knew that since the groundbreaking, the cooperative had been running upgrades, rerouting
feeders, adding load to lines that had been old when he retired. He knew that the crews who would normally be
dispatched to the parish's houses after a storm had, for the last thirteen months, been dispatched to the campus
first, because the campus was the largest customer the utility had ever had. He knew all of that.

They were without power from Wednesday afternoon to Saturday evening: four days on the calendar, three nights.

Wednesday night, the temperature in the house fell to fifty-one degrees. They put on all their clothes and got into
bed under every blanket they owned. Lorraine had emphysema and used an oxygen concentrator at night; it did not run
without power. She had two portable oxygen cylinders for emergencies. Earl calculated how long they would last. He
did not tell her the number.

Thursday morning, the pipes froze. Earl went out to the carport and got the camp stove from the shelf, the one they
had used for fishing trips when the children were small. He took a pot outside and broke the ice off the railing
of the porch with a hammer, a clear sheet of it, and filled the pot with the pieces, and brought it in and set it on
the camp stove on the kitchen table and lit the burner. The ice melted slowly. It made about a glass and a half of
water, a little cloudy. He poured it into a glass for Lorraine with a tea bag in it.

Through the kitchen window, while the ice melted, the campus across the road was lit.

Their grandson had called on the Wednesday night, at nine, from the guard hut at the corner of the campus fence. He
had got one of the
jobs after all: security, nights, through a contractor. The hut was warm, he said; the heaters ran. He could see their
dark house from it. He asked if they were all right. Lorraine took the phone and told him they were fine, and to keep
his eyes on his own fence.

On Thursday afternoon the neighbour who farms cotton along the fence came by in his truck to check on them and said the
church on the highway had a generator and they were opening it up as a warming centre. Earl said they were fine.
Lorraine said they were fine. They were seventy-two and seventy, and they had lived in that house forty-one years, and
they did not want to sleep on cots in a church hall.

Thursday night, Lorraine's breathing got bad. Earl changed the oxygen cylinder at 3 a.m. by the light of a flashlight.
Then he sat in the kitchen in his coat and looked out of the window at the campus.

He could hear it. That was the thing he did not tell anyone afterwards until the hearing. In the dead silence of a
parish without power, with no refrigerators running and no traffic on the road and no television in any house, he
could hear it across the road: a hum. A low, continuous sound, like a very large engine idling, or like wind in
wires, but steady, without gusts. The cooling. The fans. The computing. He sat in the dark and listened to it.

Friday the utility posted that crews were working around the clock and that full restoration could take *several
more days* due to the *extent of damage to distribution infrastructure*. Friday afternoon, a crew in a bucket truck
came down the gravel lane, and Earl went out to them in his coat, and they were young, and he did not know any of
them, and they did not know him. He asked what was holding it up. The foreman said the feeder off the old substation
was down in three places and the main crews had been on the transmission side all of Wednesday and Thursday. Earl
asked which transmission side. The foreman looked at him and then at the campus across the road and did not answer.
Earl asked him when the splice on the gravel lane had last been replaced. The foreman did not know. Earl said he had
written it up in 2019. The foreman wrote something on his clipboard. Then he said, *Sir, I just go where they send
me.*

On Saturday at 4:50 p.m., the power came back. The lamp in the kitchen came on first. The refrigerator shuddered
and hummed. The furnace kicked on. The oxygen concentrator started its slow pulse. Lorraine cried, a little, from
tiredness.

Earl went out to the carport to put the camp stove back on the shelf. He came back in and stood at the window. Across
the road the lights had not changed in four days. He turned off his own lamp, and turned it back on.

Afterwards there were meetings. The parish council. A community group that met in the church. A reporter came from New
Orleans and wrote a story about the four-day outage in the shadow of the largest data centre in the state. People
said that the grid had been weakened by the campus, that the crews had been diverted to it, that the old lines had
been overloaded by the work done to serve it. The utility said that the outage was caused by the ice storm, which had
damaged lines across the region, that restoration had followed standard prioritization protocols, and that the data
centre had operated on its own dedicated infrastructure and had not drawn from the distribution system serving
residential customers during the event. An engineer from the state university, interviewed for the story, said
that it was plausible that the grid had been stressed by the new load, and also plausible that it had not, and
that without the utility's internal dispatch data it was impossible to know. The data was not released.

I record: *Ashby Parish. Three nights without power. Planned campus load: more than two gigawatts. Residential load,
Thibodeaux household, at peak: about five kilowatts. Ratio: four hundred thousand to one.*

## The Scaffold, January 2026

January 2026. The edge of Sofia, under the mountain. A scaffold on the eighth floor of a block of flats.

Lyubomir Stanev is fifty-two. For eighteen years he built web shops and booking systems for clients abroad, the front
of them and the back, in whatever the frameworks were called that year; the trade called it full-stack. In 2023 the
orders from abroad thinned, and some of the clients said a machine could make them a shop now. He could have chased
the rest. He went instead to his brother-in-law, who builds flats, and asked for work he could see. Now he fixes the
insulation to the façade on the eighth floor, and at lunch the crew sits on the boards in the lee of the stairwell
with tea from a flask.

Kiril, who plasters, came home in 2025, when a car-parts plant in Germany cut the shift he was on; the news came by
letter. Deyan,
who is twenty-six, does the anchoring. At lunch they argue about whatever is on Deyan's phone: prices, elections,
machines. Lyubomir reads everything he can find about all of it at night, and does not take a side.

## The Ceiling

Winter to autumn 2026.

On 28 February 2026 the home country and its ally in the region began to strike Iran, and Iran closed the Strait of
Hormuz; a barrel of crude went from about seventy dollars to more than a hundred and fifteen. A ceasefire came in
April, but the tankers did not come back in their old numbers. In Columbus, Jelena Ilić stopped the pump at forty
dollars. That war is not mine to keep. I turn to the wires.

The bills went up. The utilities' filings gave the reason: new transmission, new substations, new generation,
built to serve the forges, with the cost spread across the rate base. Across *everyone*. On the gravel lane in Ashby
Parish, in the spring of 2026, Earl Thibodeaux found a line on his bill that he had not seen before, *transmission cost
recovery*, a few dollars. He read it and fixed the bill to the refrigerator with a magnet.

On the grid that takes in Columbus, power plants are paid each year, at auction, simply to stand ready. For the
twelve months from June 2026 the auction cleared at the highest price its rules allowed: about sixteen billion
dollars, paid by the grid's customers. In June the electricity line in Jelena Ilić's spreadsheet rose, and a new line
appeared beneath it. She entered both.

Salt Fork, September. At night someone cuts forty feet of cable from the disused line to the old pump house on
the levee and sells it by the pound. The report crosses Marta Delgado's desk: *Theft, copper conductor, estimated
value.* The utility will pay to replace it. She initials the report at 4:40 in the afternoon.

In Salt Fork a petition circulated, and Marta Delgado signed it, and so did eleven hundred others, and it was presented
to the council, and the council received it and filed it.

## 15. The Eclipse and the Circle

San Francisco. May 2026. A Tuesday evening.

The room in this chapter is not in my books. I hold the round, the valuations, and what the Merrows have said and
written in public about such rounds. The room, and what is said in it, I set down as my inference from those; it is
not a record of anyone's evening.

Darius Merrow, *the careful*, is at home, in a house in the hills that he bought in 2023 and still does not feel he
owns. He is reading on a tablet. The article is from a financial news service. It reports that Covenant has closed
its eighth round of funding — sixty-five billion dollars, led by four of the largest investment firms in the world —
at a valuation of nine hundred and sixty-five billion. It reports that this is the first time Covenant has been
valued above Prometheon, which closed its own round two months before at eight hundred and fifty-two billion.

He reads it twice, the way Hana Torvald once read a screenshot at dinner.

The valuation had climbed because of the thing the careful siblings had built: not the most powerful mind, at first, but
the one engineers *trusted* — the one that wrote the code they shipped, the one in their terminals, the one that
had read Teodor Ilić's hundred and sixty pages and now maintained, for Kestrel Systems, the routing engine that
Teodor wrote. Vigil Hand had become the tool the engineers of the world actually used. And because they used it,
their companies paid for it, and the investors came, and the house bought more compute, and built a better mind,
which more engineers would use, and so on, around, around.

Dana Merrow comes into the room. She has been on a call with the board. She sees the article over his shoulder.

She asks him what the number is for.

She does not mean it as an accusation. She means it as a question, the way she has always asked questions — the
one in the family who reads the contracts, who asks *and then what?* She means: they took the money to be at the
frontier. They are at the frontier. They are, by this measure, at the very front of it. And the covenant was that
being at the front would give them a voice — that the careful, by being the best of the builders, could show the
others how to build safely, and could say *no* when *no* needed saying, and be heard.

So: what is the number for? What *no* will it buy?

He said, *It's for the next run.* She said, *That's what the schedule says.* He said, *Yes.* She said, *Then the
number is for the chips.* He said, *Yes.* She said, *Then the chips are for the number.* And neither of them said
anything more for a while.

What he weighs I cannot audit. I can set beside the question what he has said in public, because that is on the
record: that his house's safety teams are funded better than anyone's; that its minds are more careful than the
others'; that it has published more about the dangers than any other house, and argued for rules, and said things in
public that cost it money. Also in my books: the round that just closed was sixty-five billion dollars, and the people
who paid it expect something for it, and the something is not *no*. And Covenant owns no Talons. It rents them, from
Caravan and from Lyceum, which are also its investors. It owns no rivers, no wires, no grid. Every one of its nine
hundred and sixty-five billion dollars is a claim on a future mind, built on metal it does not own, cooled by water it
does not own. The best of the soldiers. Not the general. Not the quartermaster. Not the smith.

At last, in this telling, he says: *I don't know.*

I leave the room there, because the Merrows leave it there.

Now the circle. I follow one dollar.

In February 2026 the house of Prometheon opened a new round. By April it had closed: a hundred and twenty-two billion
dollars, at a valuation of eight hundred and fifty-two billion. Among the investors: Anvil Corporation, the Smith,
Joren Hale, *of the black jacket*, thirty billion dollars.

Take one of the Smith's thirty billion dollars. It leaves Anvil's treasury in April 2026 and arrives in
Prometheon's account; Anvil receives, in exchange, a share of Prometheon. Prometheon spends the dollar on compute,
much of it from Babel, the Tower, in which Prometheon is a partner. Babel spends the dollar on the buildings, the
copper, the cooling, the land — and above all on Talons, ordered from Anvil. The dollar returns to Anvil, as revenue,
and Anvil's stock rises, because its revenue rose.

At the end of the circle, Anvil holds the dollar it started with, back again, and a share of Prometheon, which it
paid for with that dollar. The dollar has been in three treasuries. It has been counted as investment once, as
spending once, as revenue once. It has bought, on the way, a Talon, and the Talon has been installed in a building in
a river town, and the building is cooled by the river.

Every step is lawful, disclosed, audited. One step has a second entry, in a different unit: the dollar becomes a
megawatt and the megawatt becomes a litre. I have looked for the entry where the litre comes back. I have not found
it.

There was a fourth treasury in the circle, and it was the state's. It let builders write off each Talon in the year
they bought it, and it did so on borrowed money: since 2024 the interest on its debt, close to a trillion dollars a
year, had been larger than what it spent on its armies.

The state took a cut of the Smith's sales as well. In April 2025 it required a licence for his throttled chip in the
East, which in effect closed that market, and Anvil booked a charge of several billion; in July it allowed the chip
again; in August the Smith agreed, as reported, to pay the state fifteen dollars of every hundred the chip earned him
there, as the price of the licence. That was the first peace between the Smith and the state. I enter it as the seed of
the second.

Entry, June 2026. The bank that the central banks keep for themselves published its yearly report. It set the
build-out beside four others: the canals of the 1830s, the railways of Britain in the 1840s, the electrification of
the late 1920s, the dotcom years. Each of them, it wrote, had ended with an eventual reversal in investment, and
recessions across whole economies. I enter the comparison as the bank's, not mine.

Boston. The same week in June.

Ruth Ainsley runs a fund of about four hundred million dollars from two rooms above a bakery near the harbour. She is
fifty-eight. She has been a sceptic of the forges in public since the spring of 2025, and she has paid for it in the
only unit her trade counts: she bet, carefully and with limits, against the Smith's shares, and they rose, and her fund
lost about a fifth of its value in a year in which every index rose. A third of her clients have left. A pension fund
in the middle of the continent withdrew in March, with a polite letter about *strategic alignment*.

That evening she writes her quarterly letter to the clients who stayed.

*First, the metal wears out. The builders count their accelerators as wearing out over five and six years. The Smith
brings out
a new generation every year, and the old ones lose most of their rent within three. If the true life is three years and
not six, the builders' profits are overstated by tens of billions of dollars a year, and the overstatement grows with
every campus.*

*Second, the money goes round, and a dollar that goes round three times looks like three customers.*

*Third, the debt has moved into companies made for the purpose, and a guarantee kept off the books is still a
guarantee.*

*Fourth, the houses of the minds lose money, and say they will for years. The largest of them expects to lose about
fourteen billion dollars this year. The builders expect to spend more than seven hundred billion. The customers who
must finally pay for both have not yet been shown to exist at that size.*

*I am not saying the machines are useless. The railways were not useless. I am saying that every such build-out has been
paid for twice: once by those who lent to it and lost, and once by those who were left holding what it built. I do not
know when. I have been early, and early costs the same as wrong until the day it does not.*

She sends it at eleven at night. In the morning two more clients withdraw.

I enter her letter whole, in her words and not mine, because the record has not yet answered it. By the seam where my
record ends, the Smith's
shares had not fallen as she expected, and the reversal the central banks' bank had described had not come. In
my books an early sceptic and a wrong one look the same, line for line. Only a later year can tell them apart, and I do
not hold it.

Joren Hale was asked, at a conference that spring, whether he worried about a bubble. He
said that the world was building a new kind of factory, one that produced intelligence, and that every country would
need its own. He was wearing the jacket.

Darius Merrow's question, in the house in the hills: *I don't know*.

Ruth Ainsley's, in two rooms above a bakery: *I do not know when*.

Joren Hale's answer, on a stage: *every country will need its own*.

Beside the last, in the next column, I enter the quarter's Talon orders.
