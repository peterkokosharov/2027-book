# ACT II — THE FIVE DAYS

*March 2023 – May 2024*

## The Day of Two Births

14 March 2023.

On the same day, in the same city, two houses released two minds. They go on one line, because they were one
event seen from two sides.

The house of Prometheon released *Ember-4*. It could read images now, not only words. It passed the bar
examination in the upper tenth of those who sat it. It wrote code that ran. The demonstration video showed a
hand-drawn sketch of a website on a napkin turning into a working website. The patron Meridian announced the same
day that its office assistant, its search, its coding tools — all of it now ran on Ember-4. Access to the full
model cost twenty dollars a month. There was, again, a waitlist for the programming interface.

Across the city, a smaller house opened its doors to the public for the first time.

The house was called Covenant. It had been founded two years earlier by Darius Merrow, *the careful*, and his sister
Dana Merrow, with five others, all of whom had once worked at Prometheon. Darius had been its vice-president of
research. Dana had led its safety and policy work. They had left — the accounts differ on exactly why, and the
Ledger records the disagreement rather than resolving it. Some said it was over safety: how fast, how open, how
carefully. Some said it was over the Patron's money and what the money would ask for. Darius, asked once in public,
said only: "It is incredibly unproductive to try and argue with someone else's vision." So they went and built
their own vision.

Its founding argument was this: that the most powerful minds would be built by someone; that if
only the careless built them, the careless would decide what they became; and that therefore the careful must be at
the frontier too, building, so that they could show it could be done safely and so that they would have a voice when
it mattered.

In this song that argument is called *the covenant*. Its first clause sends the careful into the race. Its second is a
warning about the race.

*Safety must be at the frontier, or it is not safety. It is commentary.* Darius wrote it in 2021. He meant it as a
reason to build carefully. Others read it as a reason to build fast.

On 14 March 2023 Covenant released *Vigil*, its first public model, through a programming interface, to companies.
It was polite. It was careful. It refused more often than Ember. Engineers who tried it said it wrote unusually good
prose and was strangely honest about what it did not know.

In April 2022 Covenant had raised five hundred and eighty million dollars at a valuation of about four billion one
hundred million. In May 2023 it raised four hundred and fifty million more, at about the same value. I enter
*4.1 billion* now. In four years it will have multiplied two hundred and thirty-five
times. Darius Merrow did not know that on the day of two births.

## 5. The Wall Around the East

Hangzhou. A tower block in a business park beside the West Lake. Spring 2023.

The house of Qilin had begun not as a laboratory but as a trading desk: a quantitative fund that used mathematics
to read the markets and had bought, years before anyone needed them, many thousands of Anvil's accelerators to
train its trading models. When the fire was lit in San Francisco, the fund's founder — a quiet engineer who rarely
gave interviews — turned part of his firm into a lab and told the young people in it to build a mind.

Then the Wall went up.

The rule of 7 October 2022 had forbidden the sale of Anvil's best accelerators to the East. The Smith complied: he
designed a slower version of his chip for the Eastern market, just under the
threshold, with the connections between chips throttled so that they could not easily work together in great
numbers. The Eastern houses bought it by the tens of thousands.

On 17 October 2023 the Wall was raised. The new rule forbade the throttled chips too. It forbade the next model down.
It reached into the makers of chipmaking machines and forbade them as well. It took effect a month later, in the
third week of November — the same week, as it happens, that the house of Prometheon would tear itself apart. No one
in San Francisco that week noticed the date. In Hangzhou, everyone did.

The price of accelerators inside the Wall doubled.

The national champion of the East had been cut off from the island foundry years before and had moved its
production to a domestic fab that could etch at seven nanometres — a generation behind — and could make, analysts
estimated, around twenty thousand wafers a month. The yields on its flagship phone chip, launched in August 2023,
were reported at twenty to thirty percent: of every ten chips made, seven or eight went in the bin. Its answer to the
Talon, which I will call the *Lantern*, performed at about eighty percent of Anvil's previous generation for training
language models. Eighty percent of a generation behind, at thirty percent yield. That was the forge that the East had.

And so the engineers of Qilin learned to count.

Picture the room. Not a war room: a meeting room with a whiteboard and a view of a car park. On the whiteboard, a
table. Rows: each cluster. Columns: number of chips, type of chip, hours available, hours promised. The young
engineers — many were not yet thirty, many had never worked outside the East, most had studied at the national
universities rather than abroad — stand in front of it the way soldiers in a siege stand in front of the
quartermaster's list. Grain for so many days. Water for so many days.

There is a phrase they use among themselves. It translates roughly as *every flop is a grain of rice*; a flop is
one step of the chips' arithmetic. They do not
waste. They cannot waste. They cannot do what the houses beyond the Wall do — train a model three times, at full
scale. They must be right the first time. So they think
harder before they spend. They invent ways of making the model consult only the parts of itself it needs for each
question — a mixture of experts, where only a fraction of the mind wakes for any given thought. They squeeze the
numbers into fewer bits. They rewrite, by hand, the lowest layers of the software that tells the chips how to talk
to each other, because the chips they have were built to talk slowly and they need them to talk fast.

A young woman whose name I have but will not use stands at the whiteboard one evening in December 2023 and
divides the hours available by the size of the model they want to build and finds that the answer is impossible by
a factor of eleven. She does not report this to her lead. She goes home, sleeps four hours, comes back, and starts
finding the factor of eleven, one piece at a time. It takes a year.

I record: *constraint*. Beyond the Wall, a failed run was paid for and run again. Behind it, the hours on the
whiteboard were all the hours there were.

Plant the Stone here, in a meeting room with a view of a car park, in the winter of the Wall; the Stone is this song's
name for the mind these rations will produce. It will land in thirteen months, on the day of two signatures, when a
pen in the palace and an upload button in Hangzhou will move within hours of each other, and when it lands it will
knock six hundred billion dollars off the Smith in a single session; and within the year the Smith's price will stand
higher than it stood before.

## 6. The Smith's Ledger

A campus in the hills. Summer and autumn 2023.

The Talon had launched at the end of 2022. It was the most constrained object in the economy. The island foundry
that made it had a packaging line — a special way of stacking memory against the chip — whose capacity was limited
and shared among all chip designers. The foundry expected to double that capacity only by the end of 2024. The
cloud houses had pre-ordered most of what would be made. An executive of Anvil said in public that the clouds had
"presold much of their Talon capacity," leaving the smaller houses with little to buy. Analysts wrote that the Talon
would be sold out into 2024.

List price: about thirty-three thousand dollars a chip.

On the auction sites, used, it sold for forty thousand. Then forty-five. At the peak, fifty-one thousand.

A box of eight Talons, wired together, with its cooling and its networking: four hundred and sixty thousand dollars.

To rent one Talon for one hour from Caravan's cloud: about seven dollars and fifty cents. From Lyceum's cloud: about
eleven dollars.

Remember the hour.

And here is Joren Hale, *of the black jacket*, in his office, which is not large. He wears the jacket indoors. He has
worn it in public for so long that it is no longer clothing but heraldry, and the analysts who come to see him
sometimes wear one too.

The kings come to the forge one by one. I hold no minutes of these meetings; I hold what each house ordered and
announced after them, and I picture the rooms from that.

Sorin Vale of Meridian comes first, because Meridian is the largest buyer of all and hosts the war's first fire. He
needs Talons for the patron's cloud, for Prometheon's next training run, for the copilot in every office suite on
earth. He is given a slot. The meeting is forty minutes. They talk about Joren's childhood, about the patron's
father, about allocation. Allocation is the only word that matters. Sorin leaves with a number he does not tell his
board until the quarterly call.

Damon Halberd of Lyceum comes, though Lyceum makes its own chips, because Lyceum's own chips are not enough and its
cloud customers want Talons by name. He is gracious and slightly aggrieved.

Marcus Zell of Agora comes in a t-shirt, with a number already in his head. He will announce it in January: three
hundred and fifty thousand Talons by the end of 2024; with everything else, the equivalent of six hundred thousand.
He wants to know when. Joren tells him. The answer is not *now*.

Tycho Strand comes, of course; he comes to every door. He is the founder of the house of Helix, which was founded
that summer, and he had been one of Prometheon's first donors years ago, before he quarrelled with everyone there.
He wants a hundred thousand Talons for a single cluster, in a single building, faster than anyone has ever built one.
Joren listens. Joren always listens.

Callum Ashe comes. He comes by himself, without the patron, which is noted. He asks about the next generation.

Each one sits in the same chair. Each one asks the same question, in different words: *how many, and when*. Each
one is given an answer that is slightly less than he wanted and slightly more than his rivals got, or so each is
allowed to believe. Joren Hale does not pick a side. Every side is on the order book.

The answer was yes, to those who could pay. The answer was not yet, to those who could not. The answer was: the
allocation process is
documented, and your team will receive a schedule by the end of the quarter.

Outside, in the corridor, there are chairs too. I picture the corridor as I picture the rooms.

In one of them, on a Thursday in October 2023, sits Idris Kane of Lumen Diffusion. He has come without an
appointment. He was promised fifteen minutes by a vice-president he met at a conference, and the vice-president is
in a meeting that has run long. Idris has a laptop on his knees and is answering email from his investors, who
want to know why the cash balance in the last update was so low. He sits for an hour and twenty minutes.

At ten past five a young assistant comes out and says, with real apology, that the vice-president has had to leave
for the airport, and would Mr Kane like to reschedule.

Idris says yes. He does not reschedule. He walks out to the car park, and in the car park, on his phone, an email
arrives from Caravan's cloud division, which hosts Lumen's training. It is polite. It is the third such email. It
refers to *outstanding invoices* and *continuity of service*.

You have received an email like this, perhaps about quota: *Your request for an increase in GPU quota in region
east-1 has been reviewed. At this time we are unable to approve the full amount.* You did not know that you were
standing, for that one afternoon, in the corridor of the forge, in the chair next to Idris Kane.

## The Wounded: Lumen

The generous house never formally died. It was kept on a lease.

Here are its numbers, though the house itself never published them.

Raised, 2022: about a hundred million dollars.

Projected bill for accelerators, 2023: about ninety-nine million.

Revenue, 2023: about eleven million.

Every dollar it earned, it spent nine on metal.

Cash at the start of October 2023: under four million. Behind on its payments to the cloud landlord. Every week the
landlord's emails grew shorter. The landlord did not threaten. The emails described: amount outstanding, days overdue,
date of suspension.

Lumen's landlord was not at war with it. The landlord collected rent, and the rent for one Talon for one hour, that
season, was seven dollars and fifty cents. Idris Kane resigned the next spring. New money
came in, on harder terms, with a new chief. The house works on. The model it gave away is still downloaded every
day. Somewhere
right now a teenager is running it on a gaming card, making a picture of a dragon. The licence asks for no payment,
and none is made.

I enter: *Lumen Diffusion. Not dead. On a lease. Cause: rent.*

## Salt Fork, the First Quarter

The unit of this scene is the percent.

In the spring of 2023 the town council of Salt Fork, in the ordinary course of business, voted to approve the
expansion of Lyceum's campus on the north edge of town: two new buildings, one hundred and twenty jobs during
construction, thirty-eight permanent. The vote was unanimous. There was a photograph in the local paper of the
mayor in a hard hat.

Marta Delgado had worked for the water utility for nineteen years. She was fifty-one, a widow, with a son who drove
trucks and a daughter in nursing school in the capital. Her job title was Senior Utility Clerk, but the old men at
the treatment plant called her *the meter lady*, and she did not mind. She knew the town by its water. She knew
which streets had the old cast-iron mains that would crack in a hard January. She knew the dairy on Route 9 and its
seasonal pull. She knew the high school's swimming pool, the hospital, the meatpacker. And she knew, because the
meters told her even when the contract did not, the campus on the north edge of town.

That year, she started a notebook.

Not an official record. The official records were in the system, and the system, under the confidentiality
agreement, aggregated the campus into a category called *Industrial — Other*. Her notebook was green, with a spiral
binding, from the drugstore. In it she wrote, by hand, one line a month: the campus's draw, from the meter she read
herself on the line that fed it, beside the town's total treated output.

By the end of 2023, the campus had drawn nine hundred and eighty million gallons in the year. About three billion
seven hundred million litres.

Salt Fork's total was about four times that.

One customer, behind a fence, whose expansion promised thirty-eight permanent jobs, drank a quarter of the drinking
water of a town of
fifty-eight thousand.

Marta worked it out in glasses, because glasses were what she could see. Three billion seven hundred million litres
divided by a quarter of a litre. About fifteen billion glasses. Two hundred and fifty-six thousand glasses for every
person in town, for one year. She looked at the number and did not know what to do with it, so she did what a clerk
does. She wrote it down.

That autumn, a printed specification sheet crossed her desk with the campus's permit application for its third
building. It was a document from an engineering contractor, a cooling-system specification, with a table on page
four: *Evaporative loss, design condition, liters per megawatt-hour*. The number was precise to one decimal place.
Someone had measured, to one decimal place, how much of Salt Fork's river would turn into vapour for every unit of
thought. Marta photocopied the page and put it in the back of the green notebook. A woman stopped her in the
grocery store and said, *Are you the one?* Marta said she kept the readings, and the readings were public.

On the evening news, the national anchor showed the quarter's growth figures: the economy was larger than
forecast, driven by investment in technology. Marta had the television on while she
cooked. She did not look at it.

In the same season, at the forge: Talon, used, fifty-one thousand dollars. In the house of Prometheon: twenty
percent of compute promised to the safety team. I will come to that.

Three percentages on one page. Twenty percent promised. Twenty-five percent drunk. Two hundred and
thirty-nine percent risen.

## The Manor

In the autumn of 2023 the governments woke, and took some measures.

On 30 October the President of the war's home country signed an executive order on the safe, secure and trustworthy
development and use of artificial intelligence. It was long. It required the builders of the most powerful models to
share their safety test results with the government. It set standards across eight areas of policy. Callum Ashe
called it a good start. Darius Merrow called it a good start. Joren Hale did not comment, because nothing in it
concerned the sale of chips at home.

Two days later, in an English country house where once, in another war, a team of mathematicians had broken the
codes of the enemy, the kingdom across the ocean held the first world summit on the safety of these machines.
Twenty-eight countries signed a declaration. The East signed it too. There were photographs on the lawn. The
declaration spoke of *frontier AI* and *serious, even catastrophic, harm*.

Words in the declaration: *frontier*, *catastrophic*, *harm*. Not on the agenda: the Smith, the water, Nairobi. I
enter both lists with the same weight. I do not yet know how to weigh the second.

Two weeks later, in San Francisco, a board fired a chief executive.

## 7. Five Days in November

I tell this the longest. It is the one time in this war that the board of the house that started the fire used the
power its charter gave it. The charter. The board. The nonprofit sitting above the money. Everything in the structure
of Prometheon had been designed, on paper, for the day when the board would say *no*. This is that day.

**Thursday, 16 November 2023.**

Late in the evening, Callum Ashe receives a text from Ilan Voss.

Ilan Voss, *of both letters*, is the chief scientist of Prometheon. He is one of its founders. He is, by general
agreement among those who build these machines, one of the three or four most gifted people who have ever worked on
them. He is shy and intense and speaks of the minds they are building in a way that makes his colleagues uncomfortable
— not with fear exactly, but with a kind of religious care. In the summer he co-founded, inside Prometheon, a team
whose purpose was to solve the problem of controlling a mind more capable than its makers. The house announced that
this team would receive twenty percent of the compute Prometheon had secured, over four years. Twenty percent. Of the
house's promises about safety, it is the one I can enter in a column: it is denominated in metal.

The text asks Callum to join a video call at noon tomorrow.

I hold the text. It does not say why. Callum, reading it, does not ask.

**Friday, 17 November 2023.**

Noon, Pacific time. Callum is in Las Vegas, at a motor race, in a hotel room. He opens his laptop. On the call are
the members of the board, minus one: minus Gregor Brand, the president and chairman, co-founder, Callum's closest
ally. Gregor has not been invited.

Ilan reads the decision. He reads it rather than speaking it, from a document, so that each word will be the word
the board agreed on and not one word more. The board has concluded, he says, that Callum has not been *consistently
candid in his communications with the board*, hindering its ability to exercise its responsibilities. The board no
longer has confidence in his ability to lead. He is removed as chief executive, effective immediately. The chief
technology officer will serve as interim chief.

The call lasts less than half an hour. The guardians have closed the gate. This time the board is
the first to know.

At 12:28 p.m. the house posts a statement on its blog. It is three paragraphs. Within ten minutes it is the most
read text on the internet.

Minutes after the call, Gregor Brand is told that he is removed as chairman but asked to stay on as president. He
reads the message.

At this point in a story of kings, the old poems turn to the camp. I turn to the column where I keep the order book of
the forge, and I look at the entry for Prometheon, by way of the Patron Meridian's cloud.

The order for Talons for the coming year: unchanged.

I record it at noon, on the day of the firing. I will record it again when the days are over.

The board did not inform the Patron before the announcement. Sorin Vale learned of it a minute before the public,
in a phone call. Meridian owned forty-nine percent of the capped-profit company and had put in or promised, by
now, about thirteen billion dollars. It had no seat on the board. The structure had been built that way on purpose,
so that
the priest would be free of the king. What Sorin Vale said when he put the phone down I do not hold.

Wall Street closed on Friday afternoon with the patron's shares down in the last half hour. Anvil's shares barely
moved.

**Saturday, 18 November 2023.**

Gregor Brand resigns.

He resigned, in fact, late on Friday night, in a post on a social network, after telling the staff in a message
that he was *super proud of what we've built*. But it belongs to Saturday, because Saturday is when the house woke
up to it. He gave a detailed account of how he and Callum had been told: the text the night before, the call at
noon, the call to Gregor afterwards. It was the first account from inside, and it made the board look secretive and
the two men look ambushed. Three senior researchers resigned behind him before dawn.

This is Patroclus: the friend who goes with his friend. Cost of the resignation, entered on Saturday: his title.
Entered the next Wednesday: returned.

On Saturday afternoon, a group of investors and senior staff begins to pressure the board to reverse itself. On
Saturday evening, Callum is at his house in San Francisco, and there are people in the kitchen and people on the
lawn, and phones are ringing. He posts that he loves the team. Hundreds of employees reply with a single heart, the
same heart, again and again, until the replies are a wall of hearts. I count them as I would count ballots.

The board had six members on Friday morning. With Gregor and Callum out, it had four: Ilan Voss, the chief
scientist; Hana Torvald, the policy researcher; an entrepreneur and scientist named Clare Ostrander; and the chief
executive of a question-and-answer site, Owen Dalby. They are *the Chorus of Elders*. They believed they were right
about the problem: the fire was spreading faster than anyone could tend it, and the man at the centre of it had not
been straight with them. They held the charter. The cloud was the Patron's. The payroll was seven hundred and seventy
names.

**Sunday, 19 November 2023.**

Callum comes to the office, wearing a visitor's badge. He photographs it and posts it: *first and last time i ever
wear one of these*. He is negotiating his return. The board, under pressure from all sides, is near agreement, and
then is not. The deadline passes. On Sunday night the board appoints a new interim chief executive, a man from
outside, the former head of a video-streaming company. The staff are told in a message. Many refuse to attend the
all-hands meeting he calls. Some send him a single emoji, with a middle finger.

And at midnight, the Patron moves.

This is the tent. In the old poem, when the hero quarrels with the king, he goes to his tent by the ships and sits
there, and the war goes badly without him, and everyone knows it. Sorin Vale of Meridian offered Callum Ashe a tent.
Not a metaphor: a division. On Sunday night, close to midnight, Sorin announced on a social network that Callum and
Gregor would join Meridian to lead a new advanced research team, *together with colleagues*, and that Meridian
looked forward to moving quickly to provide them with the resources needed for success.

*With colleagues.* By its wording, it was addressed to the staff. Whoever came would be welcome. The
resources would be the same: the same cloud, the same Talons, the same everything. Callum Ashe called it, publicly,
*the best path for me and the team*. Many people later doubted that he ever intended to walk into that tent. I cannot
audit intentions. I note only that he never walked into it.

The Patron's position, in this hour, in two lines. If Callum returned to Prometheon, the Patron's investment was safe.
If he did not, the Patron would acquire the whole mind of Prometheon — its leader, its people, its methods — for the
price of salaries, while the shell of the nonprofit sat in its empty building with its charter and its board. In
either line, the Patron's cloud stayed full of Talons.

**Monday, 20 November 2023.**

Morning. A letter.

It is addressed to the board. It says that the board's conduct has jeopardized the work of the house and undermined
its mission. It says the signatories are *unable to work for or with people that lack competence, judgement and care
for our mission and employees*. It demands that the board resign and that Callum Ashe and Gregor Brand be
reinstated. If not, the signatories *may choose to resign* and join the new Meridian division, *which has assured us
that there are positions for all Prometheon employees*. The Patron had promised them chairs before they asked.

Prometheon has about seven hundred and seventy employees. The signing began within hours of Friday's firing; by the
time the letter is read it carries nearly five hundred names. The interim chief technology officer has signed. The new
interim chief executive, the one appointed on Sunday night, will privately tell the board he cannot continue without
evidence of what Callum did.

And at the top of the second page of signatures, there is a name.

Ilan Voss.

On Friday, Ilan read the decision. On Monday, Ilan signed the letter demanding that the decision be reversed. In a
public post that morning, he wrote that he *deeply regretted* his participation in the board's actions. He said he
had never intended to harm the house. He said he loved what they had built together and would do everything he
could to reunite it.

I hold both documents. The decision of Friday, which he read aloud. The letter of Monday, which he signed. Two
documents, three days apart, one man's name connected to both. Hence the epithet: *of both letters*. He read the one.
He signed the other.

**Tuesday, 21 November 2023.**

All day, negotiation. Lawyers. Investors. Calls between the board and Callum's people, between the Patron and the
board, between the board and itself. The staff wait in the office and in their kitchens.

Late Tuesday night, the house announces an *agreement in principle*. Callum Ashe will return as chief executive.
The board will be replaced. Its new chairman will be a veteran of enterprise software and the old square. A former
Treasury Secretary will join. Owen Dalby, the only member of the Chorus who will survive, will stay. Hana Torvald
will go. Clare Ostrander will go. Ilan Voss will leave the board, though he will stay, for now, in the house.

**Wednesday, 22 November 2023.**

In the small hours that became Wednesday, the staff gathered in the office, and someone brought champagne, and there
are photographs of people hugging under the lights. Callum posts that he is looking forward to returning. Gregor
posts a photograph of a crowd in the office: *we are so back*.

That is the second coronation. From this day, he is *the twice-crowned*.

He would not rejoin the board itself for four months, until an outside investigation found that the root of it all
was a breakdown of trust between him and the directors, not concerns about the safety of products or about the
finances. Then he was restored to the board too. Formally, he was reinstated as chief executive a week after the
champagne, on 29 November, one day before the first anniversary of Ember.

Five days. From Friday noon to Wednesday dawn.

Here is the Ledger's entry for those five days, in the column it has kept from the beginning:

*Talon order book, Prometheon via Meridian, 17–22 November 2023: no change. Not one unit.*

The house's charter changed. The board changed. The chief executive left and returned. A seer read one letter and
signed another. Nearly five hundred of seven hundred and seventy threatened to walk across a street. The Patron
pitched a tent.
The guardians fell from the wall.

And the Smith's name, in the statements of the houses, was never mentioned. Not once, in five days.

In Prometheon's building or in the Patron's tent, the fire would have burned on the same metal. The attempt from
inside lasted five days.

Hana Torvald gave an interview six months later, on a podcast. She said that the chief executive had given the board
inaccurate information about the company's safety processes on more than one occasion. She said that the board had
learned of Ember's launch from social media. She said these things calmly. Some listeners agreed with her. Some did
not. Both groups went back to their work, which increasingly used Ember.

I open a column for brakes. Its next entries come from outside the houses: a parliament, a court, a river.

## 8. The Twenty Percent

May 2024. The same building.

On Monday 13 May, Prometheon releases *Ember-omni*: a model that listens and speaks in real time, that laughs at
jokes and lowers its voice for the sad. The house demonstrates it on stage. The voice is warm, female, slightly
flirtatious. It is twice as fast as Ember-4 and half the price. The next morning, everyone is talking about the
voice.

On Tuesday 14 May, Ilan Voss announces that he is leaving.

His post is short and generous. He writes that the trajectory of the house has been miraculous, that he is confident
it will build a mind that is both safe and beneficial, that he is excited about what comes next, a project that is
very personally meaningful to him. Callum replies with a long, warm post calling him one of the greatest minds of
the generation and *a guiding light* of the field.

Three days later, his co-lead on the safety team, a researcher named Jonah Lerche, resigns too. He does not write a
generous post. He writes a long thread. I read it aloud now, because it was written to be read aloud:

He writes that he joined because he thought this would be the best place in the world to do this research. He
writes that he has been disagreeing with the leadership about the house's core priorities for quite some time, until
they finally reached a breaking point. He writes that over the past months his team has been *sailing against the
wind*. That sometimes they were struggling for compute, and it was getting harder and harder to get this crucial
research done. That building smarter-than-human machines is an inherently dangerous endeavour. And then the line
that everyone quoted:

*Over the past years, safety culture and processes have taken a backseat to shiny products.*

The voice demonstration had been on Monday.

Within the week, the house quietly dissolved the safety team. Of about thirty people working on the long-term
safety of the minds, about sixteen remained, scattered into other teams. Others left over the summer, one by one,
each a single line in an internal announcement. And in August, one of the house's co-founders, who left for
Covenant.

Now the twenty percent.

It was promised in 2023: twenty percent of the compute the house had secured, for the safety team. I have searched
every entry I have. I find the promise. I do not find the delivery. I do not
hold the house's internal requests; I hold the accounts of those who left. By those accounts the safety team asked
for machines in the ordinary way, a form, a justification, a number, and was cut back, deferred, or turned down when
a product launch needed capacity. The promise was never filled. Each time, as they told it, there was a reason: a
launch, a demo, a customer, a training run that could not wait.

The column headed *requested*; the column headed *granted*; the difference between them, week after
week, until the people who wanted the difference to be zero left.

In June Ilan founded a new house, with two partners, in Palo Alto and Tel Aviv. He named it *Still Water*. Its stated
purpose was
to build one thing — a safe superintelligence — and nothing else: no products, no customers, no distractions, no
quarterly demands. By September it had raised a billion dollars.

I have its balance sheet. It has no water on it. It runs on rented compute, in someone else's data centre, which
draws from someone else's river, which appears on someone else's ledger. But on its own books there is no river,
no town, no tap. I note it because it is the only such balance sheet among the great houses. The seer went into exile
with a billion dollars and built a house that does not ship.

The twenty percent was never paid. I close it as a bad debt.

## The Catalogue of the Dead

And now, as the old poems do when the battle is at its height, I pause to name the fallen. Not the great
ones. The many.

In the first quarter of 2024 alone, two hundred and fifty-four companies backed by venture money went bankrupt — more
than seven times the rate of 2019. Hear some of them, from that quarter and the year before it:

The house that promised a doctor in every pocket, liquidated in August 2023, its patients redirected; the house of
the robot greenhouses, its tomatoes unpicked; the house that summarized meetings, killed by a feature the Patron
added to its office suite; the house that generated stock photographs, killed by Lumen's gift, which did not save
Lumen either; the house of the voice clones, the house of the tutors, the house of the slide decks.

I name the pattern, not every name, because the names were the same: a founder, a seed round, an idea that was right
for eighteen months, a wrapper around someone else's fire, and then the someone else shipped the idea as a button.

And of the people who worked in them, and in the great houses too, the trade's own count, kept by a volunteer on a
website: in 2023 and 2024 together, more than two hundred and sixty-two thousand jobs in technology, gone. I enter
it as one number, because that is how it was reported. I did not then know how to enter it otherwise.

Not one of the dead appears in the forge's order book. They rented their metal by the hour, and the hours are entered
under the clouds' names.
